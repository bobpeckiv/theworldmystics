Margaret Fuller

An American editor, critic and journalist, Margaret Fuller dated her inner life from one solitary walk in 1831 and argued that the line between the sexes was never fixed.

“Male and female represent the two sides of the great radical dualism. But, in fact, they are perpetually passing into one another. Fluid hardens to solid, solid rushes to fluid. There is no wholly masculine man, no purely feminine woman.”

Margaret Fuller, Woman in the Nineteenth Century, none (written in English), 1845

At a glance

  • Born Cambridgeport, Massachusetts, May 23, 1810
  • Died in a shipwreck off Fire Island, New York, July 19, 1850
  • Tradition American Transcendentalism, raised among New England Unitarians
  • Known for the Boston Conversations, editing The Dial, and Woman in the Nineteenth Century (1845)
  • Emblem the open gate

The life

Sarah Margaret Fuller was born on May 23, 1810, in Cambridgeport, Massachusetts, where nearby Harvard College took only men. Her father, Timothy Fuller, a lawyer who later sat in Congress, resolved that his eldest child would be schooled as rigorously as any Harvard undergraduate and took charge of the lessons himself. By six she had begun Latin, and soon moved easily among several literatures. The regimen left marks. Headaches, bad dreams and sleepwalking followed her into adulthood, and she would write that she had been denied a natural childhood.

At twenty-one, on Thanksgiving Day 1831, she sat through a church service feeling walled off from everyone in the pews, then walked alone for hours across bare November fields. Beside a dark pool she passed through what she afterward described as the falling away of the separate self. She wrote the episode down in her journal in 1840 and dated her inner life from that afternoon.

Timothy Fuller died of cholera in 1835, leaving no will, and the care of her mother and younger siblings passed to her. She taught at Bronson Alcott's Temple School in Boston and then in Providence, and she translated Johann Peter Eckermann's Conversations with Goethe. In 1839 she opened her Conversations, subscription seminars at which Boston women met through the winter months to work through philosophy, mythology and the question of what a woman's life was for. From 1840 she edited The Dial, the quarterly of the circle around Ralph Waldo Emerson. Her essay "The Great Lawsuit," printed there in 1843, grew into Woman in the Nineteenth Century (1845).

Horace Greeley next brought her to New York to write for his New-York Tribune. Besides reviewing books, she visited the women held at Sing Sing and the asylum on Blackwell's Island. In 1846 the Tribune dispatched her to Europe as its correspondent. She met Thomas Carlyle in London and George Sand in Paris, came to know the exiled Giuseppe Mazzini, and in Rome in 1847 met Giovanni Angelo Ossoli, a nobleman's son about ten years her junior. Their son, Angelino, was born in 1848. No record of a marriage has ever turned up.

During the French siege of the Roman Republic in 1849, Fuller directed the hospital of the Fate Bene Fratelli on Tiber Island at the request of Princess Cristina Belgiojoso, while Ossoli served with the Civic Guard. After the republic fell, the family withdrew to Florence, where she worked on a history of the revolution. They sailed for New York in May 1850 aboard the merchant ship Elizabeth. On July 19, in a hurricane, the ship ran aground off Fire Island and broke apart within sight of the beach. All three drowned, and the history was never recovered. Henry David Thoreau walked the shore searching for their bodies and her papers. In 1852 Emerson, William Henry Channing and James Freeman Clarke published the Memoirs of Margaret Fuller Ossoli, a tribute in which they also cut and rewrote much of what she had left behind.

Core teaching

Fuller left no system, and her religious thought has to be pieced together from journals, letters and published prose, with much of the private record reaching readers only through the edited Memoirs. Two strands stand out. The first is the experience of 1831, which she understood as the end of the anxious, striving self and the start of a life lived from a larger whole. The second is the public argument of Woman in the Nineteenth Century. There she treated the masculine and the feminine not as two sealed kinds of soul but as tendencies present in every person and always shading into one another. If the division was not absolute, the customs and institutions resting on it could claim no divine sanction. Her governing principle was growth: every person, as a soul, was meant to unfold fully and without obstruction. Some readers take the book as a spiritual work in the form of social criticism; others read it chiefly as an argument about rights. Her later turn toward prisons, asylums and a wartime hospital is often read as the same conviction carried into practice.

A story told about her

One of the best-known sayings linked to Fuller is one she is not known to have said. The story goes that she once announced, "I accept the universe," and that Thomas Carlyle, hearing of it, answered, "Gad! she'd better!" No such sentence appears in her writings. The trail begins with a secondhand letter of 1848 describing a talk about her between Carlyle and Henry James Sr., with no exact words given. The tidy phrase was first pinned to her in 1862, Carlyle's retort first appeared in its short form in an 1881 article by Julia Ward Howe, and William James fixed the exchange in popular memory in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), where he offers it only as something reported. It is best read as legend: a remark about her, polished by retelling until it wore her name.

Questions to sit with

  1. Fuller dated her inner life from a single walk beside a pool. Can one afternoon reorganize a life, or does it only name a change already underway?
  2. She argued that a division nearly everyone took as natural was in fact fluid. Which divisions in your own world are treated as fixed, and what would follow if they were not?
  3. Her friends preserved her memory and reshaped her words in the same book. What do the people who outlive us owe to what we actually said?
