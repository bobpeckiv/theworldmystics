Arvo Pärt

An Estonian composer who spent eight near-silent years under Soviet rule studying medieval chant, joined the Orthodox Church, and emerged with tintinnabuli, a music built from stillness.

At a glance

  • Born Paide, Estonia, September 11, 1935
  • Tradition Christianity, in the Russian Orthodox Church, which he entered in 1972
  • Known for tintinnabuli, the spare compositional style of Für Alina, Fratres, Tabula rasa and Spiegel im Spiegel
  • Emblem the bell

The life

Arvo Pärt was born on September 11, 1935, in Paide, a small town in central Estonia, and grew up in Rakvere, where the family moved in 1938. His father left when he was two. His stepfather's house had a piano with most of its middle-register keys missing, so the boy learned to play at the high and low ends of the keyboard. He studied with the composer Veljo Tormis at the Tallinn Music School, served in the Soviet Army, and graduated in 1963 from the Tallinn State Conservatory, where he studied composition with Heino Eller. From 1958 to 1967 he worked as a sound engineer at Estonian Radio.

Estonia was then a Soviet republic, and the state controlled the orchestras, the concert halls and the composers' union. Pärt became one of the leading figures of the Soviet avant-garde. His Nekrolog (1960) was the first Estonian work written in the twelve-tone method, and in 1962 Tikhon Khrennikov, head of the Union of Soviet Composers, singled it out for criticism at a congress in Moscow. In 1968 Pärt wrote Credo, a work for choir and orchestra built around Bach's Prelude in C major and the Latin words Credo in Jesum Christum, "I believe in Jesus Christ." It was premiered in Tallinn without the union's approval. The audience demanded it again on the spot, and the work was then kept off concert programs for years.

Close to eight years of near silence followed, with one major work, the Third Symphony of 1971. The silence was partly imposed, since official commissions had dried up and film scoring was the main work left open to him, and partly a personal crisis: he later said he had lost his inner compass. He turned to Gregorian chant and to early polyphony, the Notre Dame school, Machaut, Ockeghem and Josquin, and filled notebooks by hand with single melodic lines, ten or twenty pages a day. In 1972 he married Nora Pärt, and both entered the Russian Orthodox Church.

In 1976 he wrote Für Alina, a two-minute piano piece in the new style he called tintinnabuli, from the Latin for "little bells." Fratres, Cantus in Memory of Benjamin Britten and Tabula rasa followed in 1977, and Spiegel im Spiegel in 1978. The authorities welcomed his religious music no more than his modernism, and in January 1980 the family emigrated, first to Vienna and, from 1981, to Berlin on a DAAD scholarship. In 1984 the German label ECM launched its New Series with Tabula rasa, and his music found a world audience. By the 2000s he was among the most performed living composers.

Estonia regained its independence in 1991. The Pärts returned to live there for good in 2010 and founded the Arvo Pärt Centre, now at Laulasmaa on the Gulf of Finland, which keeps his archive. He turned ninety in September 2025.

Core teaching

Pärt has turned down the label "mystic." Asked about the word in a 2014 NPR interview, he rejected it and drew a line, which he credited to the sobriety of Eastern Christianity, between mysticism and mystification. What he offers is less a doctrine than a method. Tintinnabuli is a strict rule system: one voice moves step by step through a melody, while a second voice sounds the nearest note of the home chord. The two are heard as a single line. Pärt has described the pairing in religious terms: "One line is who we are, and the other line is who is holding and takes care of us" (to Arthur Lubow, The New York Times Magazine, 2010). For him the technique and the prayer are one act. Silence is the other key. He has compared it to a blank sheet, fertile ground to be approached with awe, and has called inner silence harder to reach than outer quiet. He has said that the Orthodox faith reached him through the Desert Fathers, and he has set the words of Saint Silouan the Athonite in Silouan's Song and Adam's Lament.

A story told about him

In 2007, accepting an honorary doctorate in theology in Freiburg, Pärt told a story from his years of searching. Unsure how music should be written at all, he had begun putting the question to anyone he met, and one of the people he asked was a street-sweeper. The man answered that a composer would probably need to love each and every sound. Pärt told the audience that this plain answer was a turning point, and that from then on his musical thinking moved in an entirely new direction. He had begun the speech by saying he did not feel like a theologian. He ended the story with a question about the sweeper and an answer of his own: "If he was a theologian, then I wanted to be one, too" (trans. Laura Neill).

Questions to sit with

  1. Pärt wrote almost nothing for close to eight years before finding the style he is known for, and part of that silence was forced on him. When has stepping back from something led somewhere new, and how can anyone tell that apart from simple loss?
  2. His music follows a strict rule, yet many listeners hear it as free and prayerful. Can a discipline or constraint make room for something, rather than only limit it?
  3. He separates mysticism from "mystification," which he associates with exaltation and fantasy. How might a person tell real depth from mere atmosphere?
