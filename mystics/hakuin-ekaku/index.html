Buddhism · 1686–1769 · Hara, Suruga Province, Japan

Hakuin Ekaku

A Japanese Zen priest who did more than anyone to renew the Rinzai school, through rigorous *kōan* training, sermons for ordinary people and thousands of ink paintings.

At a glance

  • Born Hara, Suruga Province, Japan, at the foot of Mount Fuji, in 1686
  • Died Hara, in 1769
  • Tradition Buddhism, in the Rinzai school of Japanese Zen
  • Known for renewing kōan practice, the kōan of the sound of one hand, thousands of Zenga ink paintings and his account of Zen sickness
  • Emblem the ensō

The life

Hakuin Ekaku was born in 1686, the youngest son of a family that kept a rural inn in Hara, below Mount Fuji in Suruga Province. In the Tokugawa era the state had drawn Buddhism into the work of government: every household had to register with a temple, and many priests served as local record keepers. Rinzai Zen had by many accounts grown comfortable and ceremonial over the preceding centuries. The kōan, the paradoxical question a student works on in meditation, was often handled as a puzzle with a set answer.

As a boy he heard a priest describe the Eight Hot Hells of Buddhist cosmology, and the fear of damnation would not leave him. He entered the priesthood in his early teens at Shōin-ji, the temple in his home village, hoping, he later wrote, to find a way out of hell. Years of pilgrimage followed. At twenty-four, during a retreat at Eigan-ji, the sound of a temple bell at dawn brought his first awakening, and by his own later account he believed his insight had no equal in three hundred years.

That confidence did not survive Shōju Rōjin, a severe teacher with whom he spent eight months. Shōju rejected his understanding with harsh words and blows, and Hakuin came to regard him as his true master. Tradition counts Hakuin as Shōju's heir, and Shōju urged him to stay on as successor, which he declined; no formal transmission is documented.

Further breakthroughs came, but the strain of forcing them broke his health. He described a burning head, icy feet, ringing ears and constant fear, a condition he called Zen sickness. In his own telling, a Taoist hermit named Hakuyū taught him to draw breath and attention down into the lower belly, and he recovered. From then on he treated care of the body as part of practice.

In his early thirties he returned to Shōin-ji, by then poor and run down, became its head priest and served there for roughly fifty years. Around forty-one, while he was absorbed in the Lotus Sutra, a scripture he had earlier dismissed, the chirp of a cricket brought the realization he regarded as decisive.

His remaining decades went to teaching monks, farmers, samurai and illiterate villagers. He wrote letters of practical advice, collected as Orategama (The Embossed Tea Kettle), and told his own story in works such as Wild Ivy (Itsumadegusa). Near sixty he took up the brush in earnest and made thousands of Zenga, ink paintings of Bodhidharma, the laughing monk Hotei and blind men crossing a log bridge. He devised the kōan of the sound of one hand, which sets the sound of two hands clapping against a single hand raised in silence. He died in Hara in 1769. Virtually every Rinzai Zen teacher today traces their line back to Hakuin, through Gasan Jitō and Gasan's students Inzan Ien and Takujū Kosen, though the historian Heinrich Dumoulin doubted that Gasan was formally Hakuin's heir.

Core teaching

Hakuin's Song of Zazen (Zazen Wasan) opens by comparing beings and the Buddha to ice and water: ice is already water, and beings are already Buddha. Those who search for truth far away, the poem says, are like someone standing in water and crying out with thirst. Its closing lines name this very place as the Lotus Land and this very body as the Buddha. Hakuin did not take this as a reason to stop practicing. He held that awakening has to be deepened again and again, carried by what he called great faith, great doubt and great resolve, and he used kōan training to bring students to kenshō, seeing into their own nature. His illness taught him that insight chased in the head alone can wound the body, so he rooted practice in the breath and the tanden, the center of energy in the lower belly. And he placed practice in the middle of daily life. Passing on a teaching of the Chinese master Dahui, he wrote that "the method of the life of activity is a million times better than the method of calm" (Orategama, trans. R. D. M. Shaw).

A story told about him

As Hakuin tells it, the hermit Hakuyū gave him a cure for Zen sickness so gentle it seemed almost silly for a man who had been at war with himself. He was to picture a lump of soft, fragrant ointment, like butter, set on top of his head. Warmed by the body, it would slowly melt and seep downward through his head, shoulders, chest and belly, on through the legs to the soles of the feet, until the whole body was soaked and the heat that had risen into his head drained away. He kept at it, and within three years, he wrote, his old illnesses were gone. Years later, at the request of his monks, he wrote the method down in Yasen Kanna so that others worn down by practice could use it.

Questions to sit with

  1. Hakuin's search began with a child's terror of hell. Can fear be a real beginning for a spiritual life, or must it be outgrown before anything else can take root?
  2. He drove himself toward awakening so hard that his body gave way, and he recovered only by easing off. Where does effort help, and where does it get in its own way?
  3. The Song of Zazen compares people who seek truth far away to someone crying for water while standing in it. What might be close at hand that is easy to overlook?
