Hinduism

The third-largest faith on earth has no single founder, creed or central authority. Here are the basics: where it came from, what Hindus teach, how they practice, and who to meet first.

At a glance

  • Followers about 1.2 billion, mostly in India and Nepal
  • Sacred texts the Vedas, the Upanishads, the epics and the Puranas
  • Major traditions Vaishnava, Shaiva, Shakta and Smarta

One name, many traditions

"Hindu" began as an outsider's word. Persians, and later Europeans, applied it to the peoples east of the Indus, and "Hinduism" became common English only in the nineteenth century. Many Hindus prefer Sanatana Dharma, "the eternal way."

Under that name live many traditions. Vaishnavas worship Vishnu, including as Krishna and Rama. Shaivas worship Shiva. Shaktas worship the Goddess, Devi, as Durga, Kali and more. Smartas honor several deities as forms of one reality. Across all of these run paths: bhakti (devotion), yoga (disciplined practice) and Vedanta (philosophy rooted in the Upanishads). One household may draw on several. This page ranks none of them.

Origins

The oldest Hindu texts are the Vedas, Sanskrit hymns kept by memory and recitation for many generations before they were written. Many Hindus teach that the Vedas have no human author; ancient seers, the rishis, heard them. Historians place the earliest hymns around 1500–1000 BCE.

Later came the Upanishads, which ask what the self really is; the Mahabharata and Ramayana epics; the Puranas, full of stories of the gods; and poet-saints singing in their own regional languages. Since the 1800s Hindus have also settled across the Caribbean, Africa, Europe and North America.

Core ideas

The Upanishads speak of Brahman, ultimate reality, and atman, the deepest self. Philosophers disagree about how they relate. Shankara taught that they are one. Ramanuja taught that the self is distinct from God yet inseparable, and Madhva that the two stay forever distinct. Ideas Hinduism shares with other traditions include nonduality, the witness, divine love and surrender.

Dharma is right conduct and cosmic order, shaped by one's place and season in life. Karma teaches that actions carry consequences forward, samsara is the cycle of rebirth, and moksha is liberation from it.

Hindus honor many gods and goddesses. Many see them as faces of a single divine reality; others hold one deity supreme. That is why many Hindus object to the outsider's label "polytheism."

The Bhagavad Gita, a 700-verse dialogue within the Mahabharata, is probably the best-known Hindu text abroad. Before a battle, Prince Arjuna loses heart, and Krishna, his charioteer, teaches him three paths: selfless action, knowledge and devotion. Many yoga teacher trainings read it.

Practices

Worship, called puja, happens at home shrines and in temples, with offerings of flowers, food, light and incense before a murti, a sacred image in which, Hindus teach, the deity is present. Darshan is the exchange of sight between worshipper and deity.

Diwali celebrates light, Holi greets spring with colored powder, and Navaratri gives nine nights to the Goddess. Pilgrims bathe in the Ganges at Varanasi. Rites mark birth, marriage around a sacred fire, and cremation at death.

In the West "yoga" often means postures. In the tradition it is wider: a discipline of union. Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, usually dated to the early centuries CE, list eight limbs, and posture is one. Hindus debate modern yoga too. Some ask that its roots be acknowledged; others see it as open to all.

Indian society has long been divided into birth groups (jati), and some old texts describe four classes (varnas). India's 1950 Constitution abolished "untouchability," Hindu reformers have long opposed caste ranking, and how caste relates to the religion is still argued, among Hindus as well.

Key figures

  • Shankara (8th century): teacher of Advaita Vedanta, the nondual reading of the Upanishads
  • Ramanuja (11th–12th centuries): a Vaishnava philosopher who joined Vedanta to devotion
  • Akka Mahadevi (12th century): a Kannada poet who addressed every poem to Shiva
  • Kabir (c. 1440–c. 1518): a weaver of Benares whose songs challenged Hindu and Muslim custom, and whom both communities claim
  • Mirabai (c. 1498–c. 1546): a Rajput princess whose songs to Krishna are sung to this day
  • Ramakrishna (1836–1886): a Bengali devotee of Kali who taught that many paths reach one goal
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902): his disciple, who brought Vedanta to Chicago in 1893
  • Mahatma Gandhi (1869–1948): leader of nonviolent resistance, lifelong reader of the Gita
  • Ramana Maharshi (1879–1950): a south Indian sage who taught self-inquiry: "Who am I?"
  • Anandamayi Ma (1896–1982): a Bengali holy woman revered across traditions
