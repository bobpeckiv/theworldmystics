Sikhism · 1563–1606 · Goindval to Lahore, Punjab

Guru Arjan

Guru Arjan, the fifth Sikh Guru, compiled the volume that grew into the Guru Granth Sahib, setting beside the Gurus' hymns the songs of saints from other castes and faiths.

“O my soul, why proposest thou exertion when God Himself is engaged in effort for thee? He even putteth their food before the insects which He created in rocks and stones.”

Guru Arjan, Rag Gujari, from the Rahiras, Max Arthur Macauliffe, 1909

At a glance

  • Born 1563, at Goindval, a river town in the Punjab
  • Died 1606, at Lahore, in Mughal custody
  • Tradition Sikhism, as its fifth Guru
  • Known for compiling the Adi Granth in 1604; building the Harmandir at Amritsar; the Sukhmani
  • Emblem woven cloth

The life

Arjan was born at Goindval, beside the Beas, in 1563, into the family that had come to lead the Sikh community. His mother, Bibi Bhani, was the daughter of the third Guru, Amar Das, and his father, Ram Das, became the fourth. The Punjab lay within the Mughal empire, and for most of Arjan's life the emperor was Akbar, whose court was known for its openness to many religions. The community Guru Nanak had founded was spreading across the province and building a new town around a pool at Amritsar.

Ram Das had three sons. In 1581 he passed over the eldest, Prithi Chand, and named Arjan, the youngest, as his successor. Prithi Chand never accepted it. Sikh sources say he ran a rival establishment, diverted offerings meant for the Guru, and put his own verses about as though they were scripture.

Arjan was a builder and organizer. He completed the pool at Amritsar and raised at its center the Harmandir, the shrine now called the Golden Temple. It has an entrance on each of its four sides, and its floor lies below the surrounding ground, so that those who come in step down. He founded the towns of Tarn Taran, Kartarpur and Hargobindpur, had wells dug, and organized the masands, agents who gathered a fixed share of the followers' income for the community's work.

His largest undertaking was a book. In 1604, with his mother's cousin Bhai Gurdas as scribe, he compiled the Adi Granth. It brought together the hymns of the first four Gurus, over two thousand compositions by Arjan himself, and verses by bhagats, saints outside the Sikh fold, among them Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas and the Sufi Sheikh Farid. Collections made at Goindval under Guru Amar Das had already set some of these voices among the Gurus' hymns. Arjan ordered the whole by raag, the musical mode in which a hymn is sung, not by its author's standing, and one authorized text left counterfeit verses nowhere to circulate.

Jahangir succeeded his father Akbar in 1605. The next year Jahangir's son Khusrau rose in revolt and, in flight, passed through the Guru's community. The two sides remember the encounter differently. Sikh sources describe the ordinary hospitality given to any traveler. Jahangir's memoir says the Guru marked the prince's forehead with saffron, and that Jahangir had long wanted the Guru's influence ended. Arjan was called to Lahore and his property seized, and he died there in 1606; sources give different days in May and June. The memoir records the emperor's order. Sikh tradition remembers days of torture and a final bathe in the Ravi from which he never came back, details absent from the memoir, and later tradition adds that he was pressed to alter the scripture and refused. How far his death was a political punishment and how far a religious one is still argued. Sikhs honor him as the first martyr of their faith, and his son Hargobind became the sixth Guru.

Core teaching

Guru Arjan's hymns, signed like those of every Guru with the name Nanak, return again and again to one God who pervades all things and cannot be confined to any temple, rite or community. Because that God is present in everyone, caste and creed carry no spiritual weight, a conviction the Adi Granth itself enacts by giving low-caste and Muslim saints a place in it. The path he taught is naam simran, steady remembrance of the divine Name, kept up in the midst of work and family life and strengthened in the sadh sangat, the company of the holy. Its fruit is a quiet acceptance of hukam, the divine order, and a release from envy and enmity. In one hymn in Raag Kanra he reports that jealousy has simply left him, so that he can find neither enemy nor stranger anywhere, since the One is present in all. His longest work, the Sukhmani, the "Jewel of Peace," is among the most widely recited compositions in Sikh devotion.

A story told about him

When the Adi Granth was completed in 1604, it was installed in the Harmandir, with Baba Buddha, an elder who had known Guru Nanak himself, as its first reader. The story goes that Arjan had the volume set on the raised seat reserved for the Guru and took his own place on the ground beneath it, and that at night he lay down to sleep at its foot rather than above it. Tellings vary in detail, but the gesture has become one of the tradition's best-loved images of what the book would later become in name: in 1708 the tenth Guru, Gobind Singh, declared that the scripture, not any human successor, would be Guru after him.

Questions to sit with

  1. Arjan placed hymns by a weaver, a leatherworker and a Sufi in the same book as his own, ordered by music rather than by rank. Whose voices are missing from the books and teachers you treat as authoritative, and why?
  2. His elder brother contested his place for years and circulated verses under his name. How do you carry a grievance that arises inside your own family or community?
  3. In one hymn he describes jealousy not as something he defeated but as something he found had gone. What would it mean to notice the absence of a resentment rather than to fight it?
