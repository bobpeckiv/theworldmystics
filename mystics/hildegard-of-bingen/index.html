Christianity · 1098–1179 · the Rhineland, Germany

Hildegard of Bingen

Enclosed in a monastery cell as a child, she kept her visions hidden for decades, then wrote nine books, composed seventy-seven songs, and wrote to popes and emperors.

At a glance

  • Born Bermersheim vor der Höhe, in the Rhineland, 1098
  • Died Rupertsberg, near Bingen, September 17, 1179
  • Tradition Christian mysticism, in the Benedictine order
  • Known for her visionary books, her music, her writing on medicine, and the word viriditas, "greening"
  • Emblem the wax tablet

The life

Hildegard was born in 1098, the tenth child of a family of lower nobility in the Rhineland. At about eight her family gave her to the Church, placing her in the care of an older woman, Jutta von Sponheim; in 1112 the two were enclosed together in a cell at the Benedictine monastery of Disibodenberg. She had been seeing visions of light since before she was five. For decades she told only Jutta and a monk named Volmar.

After Jutta's death in 1136, the women of the community chose Hildegard as their leader. Five years later, at forty-two, she had a vision commanding her to record what she saw. She resisted and fell ill, and she began to write. Volmar served as her scribe, and a young nun named Richardis von Stade helped her. The book took about ten years. She called it Scivias, "Know the Ways."

Word of her spread before the book was finished, and by the end of the 1140s the most powerful men in the Church had taken her side. In 1150 she moved her nuns to a new monastery at Rupertsberg, against the wishes of her abbot, Kuno of Disibodenberg. In 1165 she founded a second house at Eibingen, across the Rhine.

She wrote as if she had been storing it up. There were more visionary books, including the Book of Divine Works. There were seventy-seven or more songs for worship and the Ordo Virtutum, the first known morality play. She wrote on medicine, plants, and the natural world, and she invented a language of her own, the Lingua Ignota. The idea that held all of it together was viriditas, "greening," her word for the living force she saw moving through everything that grows.

In her sixties and seventies she traveled and preached in public, which almost no woman of her time did. She wrote to popes and emperors. Late in life, when church officials ordered her to dig up a nobleman buried in her churchyard, she refused and defended herself in a letter to the leaders of the church at Mainz. In 2012, more than eight centuries after her death at Rupertsberg, Pope Benedict XVI declared her both a saint and a Doctor of the Church.

Core teaching

The idea that ties Hildegard's work together is viriditas, a Latin word for greenness that she made her own. For her it named the living force in everything that grows: moisture in leaves, sap in trees, blood in the body, fruitfulness in fields and in people. In her visions the divine does not stand apart from the world, watching from above. It is the world's aliveness, one fire burning in Creator and creation alike, though she never treats the two as simply the same. She saw the human being as a small image of the whole cosmos, a theme her Book of Divine Works develops at length. This is why the body mattered in her thought. She wrote on herbs, stones and healing alongside theology and music because she saw the divine life working through matter, not hovering above it. In her images, drying up stood for sin and despair, while staying green and flourishing stood for holiness.

A story told about her

Around 1147, news of a nun at Disibodenberg who saw visions reached the highest levels of the Church. In the twelfth century a woman claiming to hear from God could be dismissed or silenced. Instead, her case went before Bernard of Clairvaux, the most respected monk in Europe, and he lent her his support. Then Pope Eugene III, meeting with bishops at the Synod of Trier, gave her visions his approval. A woman who had kept quiet for most of her life now wrote with the backing of a pope, and she did not go quiet again.

Questions to sit with

  1. In 1150 Hildegard moved her nuns to a new monastery even though her abbot did not want her to go. When is it right to go against the person who has authority over you, and what should you be ready to lose if you do?
  2. Her work was taken seriously only after powerful men approved it. Was she right to seek that approval, or did it give them too much control over her voice?
  3. Hildegard wrote about religion, music, medicine, and plants, and she did not treat them as separate subjects. What do people gain when they connect fields of knowledge, and what might they lose?
