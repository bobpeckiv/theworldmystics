Walt Whitman

A Brooklyn printer and newspaperman who belonged to no church, Whitman spent thirty-seven years rewriting one book, *Leaves of Grass*, and three years visiting Civil War hospital wards.

“Swiftly arose and spread around me the peace and knowledge that pass all the argument of the earth, / And I know that the hand of God is the promise of my own, / And I know that the spirit of God is the brother of my own, / And that all the men ever born are also my brothers, and the women my sisters and lovers, / And that a kelson of the creation is love,”

Walt Whitman, "Song of Myself," section 5, Leaves of Grass (Philadelphia: David McKay), original English, 1891–92 final edition, 1891–92

At a glance

  • Born West Hills, Long Island, New York, May 31, 1819
  • Died Camden, New Jersey, March 26, 1892
  • Tradition none; a poet outside every church, shaped by the Quaker preaching of Elias Hicks and by the essays of Ralph Waldo Emerson
  • Known for Leaves of Grass, first issued in 1855 and reworked through six major editions, and some three years spent in the military hospitals of wartime Washington
  • Emblem the composing stick

The life

Walter Whitman Jr. was born on May 31, 1819, at West Hills on Long Island, the second of nine children. His father, a carpenter, moved the household to Brooklyn and then from one rented house to the next as his ventures failed. His parents admired Elias Hicks, the Quaker preacher, and took the boy to hear him. School ended when Walt was eleven. He became an office boy, and at twelve he entered a print shop as an apprentice compositor, picking up type by hand.

For twenty years he worked in the trades of words: compositor, teacher in one-room Long Island schools, reporter and editor. He ran the Brooklyn Daily Eagle until 1848, when he lost the post partly over his free-soil politics. His early rhymed verse and temperance fiction gave little hint of what followed. Around 1850 he began filling notebooks with long unrhymed lines, a change no biographer has fully explained.

Leaves of Grass came out in 1855, paid for by its author: twelve untitled poems in ninety-five pages. The title page named no poet. Opposite it stood an engraved portrait of a bearded figure in shirtsleeves, hat on, one hand on his hip. Few copies sold, though Ralph Waldo Emerson answered with a letter of high praise. Whitman kept revising it until he died.

The Civil War redirected him. In December 1862 a casualty list from Fredericksburg carried a garbled version of his brother George's name, and Walt traveled south to search. He found George at the Union camp near Falmouth, Virginia, with only a minor wound, but what he saw at the field hospital there kept him from going home. He settled in Washington, lived on government clerkships, and for about three years gave his spare hours to the military wards, bringing small comforts and writing letters for men unable to write their own. His Memoranda During the War (1875) put the total at over six hundred visits and between eighty and a hundred thousand meetings with sick and wounded men; the estimate is his own and counts meetings, not patients nursed. In June 1865 James Harlan, the Interior Secretary, came across Whitman's own copy of Leaves of Grass at the office, found it obscene, and fired him. Within a day friends had placed him in the Attorney General's office.

A stroke in January 1873 left him partly paralyzed, and his mother died that May, a loss that hit him harder still. He went to live in Camden, New Jersey, where he became the Good Gray Poet, looked after by younger admirers. One of them, Horace Traubel, recorded his talk from 1888 onward. In one recorded conversation the old poet predicted that Black and Native Americans would vanish, and scholars have traced how his racial views shifted with politics and hardened late in life. He died in Camden on March 26, 1892, at seventy-two, months after approving the final edition of his book.

Core teaching

Whitman claimed no doctrine, and his religious vision has to be read out of the poems. "Song of Myself" opens by celebrating the self and then hands that self straight to the reader: whatever the poet assumes, the reader will assume, and every atom of his belongs to the reader too. The self being praised is less a private biography than something wide enough to hold everyone. His long catalogues carry the idea in their very form. Pilots, deacons, prostitutes, presidents and a fugitive from slavery are named in one tone and given equal space, so rank dissolves through rhythm instead of argument. He calls himself the poet of the Body and of the Soul and will not place either one higher, asking in "I Sing the Body Electric" what the soul could be if the body were not it. Readers have often compared this to Vedanta, but the likeness is convergence, not inheritance. When Henry David Thoreau visited in 1856 and asked if he had read the Orientals, Whitman said no; Thoreau wrote afterward that the poems were strikingly close to them.

A story told about him

The story most often told about the first Leaves of Grass concerns a letter. On July 21, 1855, Ralph Waldo Emerson, then the best-known essayist in the country, wrote from Concord to the unknown Brooklyn poet. "I greet you at the beginning of a great career," the letter said, "which yet must have had a long foreground somewhere for such a start." Whitman did not ask permission to use it. He had the letter reprinted in the New York Tribune and slipped copies into unsold books. When a second edition appeared in 1856, the line about a great career was stamped in gold on its spine, with Emerson's name beneath it. A private word of encouragement had become a cover blurb.

Questions to sit with

  1. Whitman celebrated himself and in the same breath gave everything he claimed away to the reader. Can high regard for oneself and generosity toward others be the same act?
  2. Much of his war work was small: a stamp, an orange, a letter, an hour beside a bed. What small act has mattered most to you at a time when you needed help?
  3. The poet who wrote every sort of American into his lines also said things about race, late in life, that are hard to square with them. How can a reader hold a person's finest work and worst words at once?
