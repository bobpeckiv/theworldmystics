Santee Dakota · 1858–1939 · Minnesota to Detroit

Charles Eastman

A Dakota boy raised in Canadian exile who became a Boston-trained doctor, treated the survivors of Wounded Knee, and wrote books explaining his people's faith to America.

At a glance

  • Born near Redwood Falls, Minnesota, February 19, 1858
  • Died Detroit, Michigan, 1939
  • Tradition Santee Dakota (Wahpeton); the religion of Wakan Tanka, the Great Mystery
  • Known for books, written in his own English, explaining Dakota religion to non-Native readers
  • Emblem a lacrosse stick

The life

Charles Eastman began life as Hakadah, "the pitiful last," born near Redwood Falls, Minnesota, on February 19, 1858, the youngest of five children. His mother died before his first birthday, and his father's mother, Uncheedah, a respected healer, took him in. After the Dakota lost the US-Dakota War of 1862, she carried the four-year-old across the border into Canada. His father, Many Lightnings, and two older brothers were handed over to American authorities, and word came that they had been hanged.

In Canada he learned the old disciplines: the bow and the hunt, the sweat lodge, the practice of silence, and an unspoken prayer each day to Wakan Tanka, the Great Mystery. A lacrosse win by his band earned him the name that outlasted every other, Ohiyesa, "the Winner." In September 1873, after a decade of mourning, the fifteen-year-old came back from hunting to find Many Lightnings alive in camp. Four years in prison had made his father a Christian, and he now farmed at Flandreau in Dakota Territory as Jacob Eastman. Uncheedah had taught the boy to distrust the white man's road. By his own account, filial loyalty alone carried him south.

The father sent him to Alfred Riggs's mission school in Santee, Nebraska. In 1876, as the young man was leaving for Beloit College, word came that Jacob Eastman had died suddenly. Father and son had shared three years. Eastman pressed on through Knox College and Kimball Union Academy to Dartmouth, finishing in 1887. Boston University granted him an M.D. in 1890, placing him among the earliest Native Americans to qualify as physicians in Western medicine.

That November he became agency physician at Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota. On December 29, 1890, the Seventh Cavalry killed hundreds of Lakota at Wounded Knee Creek. Eastman turned the agency's mission chapel into a field hospital, and three days later he guided a search party over the snow-covered site, where a few people were still found alive. His later account of that winter is a key eyewitness record. Elaine Goodale, a supervisor of Sioux schools, married him in June 1891.

His medical posts at Pine Ridge (to 1893) and later at Crow Creek both ended in clashes with officials he judged corrupt. He spent six years assigning English legal surnames to Sioux families, worked for the YMCA, advised the Boy Scouts of America, and worked with the Society of American Indians, a national Native organization. With Elaine editing, his books ran from Indian Boyhood (1902) to Indian Heroes and Great Chieftains (1918). The Soul of the Indian (1911) argued that Dakota religion needed no shrine but the natural world, no clergy, and no weekly holy day, because all time was sacred. The couple separated for good in 1921. He spent his last years lecturing and retreating to a cabin he built in the woods, and died of heart failure in 1939, during a visit to his son in Detroit.

Core teaching

In The Soul of the Indian (1911), Eastman argued that the Dakota understanding of the sacred had already reached what contemplatives in many traditions seek, without a church, a creed or an ordained clergy. Wakan Tanka, the Great Mystery, is in his account not one being among others but the surrounding reality in which all life is lived, and traditional Dakota religion was arranged to keep each person in direct contact with it. Worship, he wrote, was silent and solitary, because speech falls short of the sacred, and no one was to stand between another person and that experience. Its central discipline was the vigil he called bambeday, in which a youth climbed to a high, lonely place and kept still and silent there through a day and night or more, with no request to make. Ordinary devotion followed the same pattern, with each person greeting the dawn without a go-between. He treated animals and the land as kin and held all days as equally sacred. He also wrote that the spirit of Christianity and of the old Dakota religion were essentially the same, and that the civilization calling itself Christian had not lived by it.

Quote

"Each soul must meet the morning sun, the new, sweet earth, and the Great Silence alone!"

—Charles Eastman (Ohiyesa), The Soul of the Indian: An Interpretation (1911), chapter II, "The Family Altar." Written in English by the author.

Questions to sit with

  1. Eastman grew up in the old Dakota way and then spent years in American schools and colleges. Did he have to give up one world to gain the other? What in his life points toward an answer?
  2. For six years his job was giving Sioux families new legal surnames, and he had carried several names himself. What does a name do for a person, and who should get to decide it?
  3. He wrote that each person must meet the morning "alone," with no one standing in between. What might a tradition gain by keeping go-betweens out of a person's religious life, and what might it lose?
