Taoism · by tradition 6th century BCE · Zhou China

Laozi

Tradition credits this legendary sage with the Tao Te Ching, a short book on the nameless Way and unforced action; whether he ever lived is uncertain.

“The highest excellence is like (that of) water. The excellence of water appears in its benefiting all things, and in its occupying, without striving (to the contrary), the low place which all men dislike. Hence (its way) is near to (that of) the Tao.”

Laozi, Tao Te Ching, ch. 8, James Legge, 1891

At a glance

  • Born unknown; tradition places him in the sixth century BCE, as an older contemporary of Confucius
  • Died unknown; the story says he left China through the Hangu Pass and was not seen again
  • Tradition Taoism, as its founding figure, later revered as a deity in religious Taoism
  • Known for the Tao Te Ching, the Book of the Way and Its Power

The life

Whether Laozi was a historical person is an open question, and most modern scholars treat him as a figure of legend. The name is a title of respect meaning "Old Master." What survives is a book, the Tao Te Ching, and a body of stories that grew up around the man said to have written it.

The fullest early account of his life comes from the historian Sima Qian, whose Records of the Grand Historian dates from around 100 BCE, centuries after the time tradition assigns to Laozi. The historian was himself unsure. He gave the sage's family name as Li and his personal name as Er, and said he kept the archives of the royal court of the Zhou dynasty, but he also offered other candidates for the name. Tradition makes Laozi an older contemporary of Confucius. Their meeting is already told in the earlier book of Zhuangzi, and Sima Qian repeats it: Confucius, the story goes, left the encounter comparing Laozi to a dragon, a creature beyond his grasp.

The best-known episode is the departure. Seeing the Zhou order in decline, Laozi is said to have left the court and set out west. At the Hangu Pass on the frontier, the keeper of the pass, Yinxi, knew who the traveler was and asked that he record his teaching before going on. Laozi set down some five thousand Chinese characters in two parts, then passed through the gate, and where he ended his days no one could say. Later paintings show him riding a water buffalo, a detail that does not come from Sima Qian. In religious Taoism he was eventually honored as a god, Taishang Laojun.

Modern scholarship tells a different story about the book. The Tao Te Ching appears to have grown over time out of older sayings, shaped by more than one hand. Bamboo slips excavated at Guodian in 1993, from a tomb of about 300 BCE, preserve roughly two-fifths of the received text in an earlier form. Silk manuscripts recovered at Mawangdui in 1973, from a tomb sealed in 168 BCE, place the section on de, "virtue" or "power," ahead of the section on the Tao. The familiar division into eighty-one chapters came later. The text was evidently still changing in the fourth and third centuries BCE.

The world the book speaks to is the long collapse of Zhou rule, when rival states made war on one another and competing thinkers, remembered as the Hundred Schools, each offered rulers a cure. The followers of Confucius prescribed ritual, rank and duty. The Tao Te Ching asked whether the whole effort to force the world into order was itself the trouble. With the Zhuangzi, it became one of the two founding texts of Taoism, it fed into Chan and Zen Buddhism, and it is now one of the most widely translated books in the world.

Core teaching

The opening lines of the Tao Te Ching declare that the Tao, the Way of all things, lies beyond words; any name given to it falls short. The rest of the book points toward the Tao through paradox and image rather than definition. The Tao is not one thing among others but the source and pattern of everything, prior to the distinctions language draws. From this comes wu wei, usually rendered "non-action": not idleness, but action so in keeping with how things move that it never forces. Water is the book's favorite example. It is soft and yielding, it settles in the low places others avoid, and in time it wears through stone. Opposites such as hard and soft, full and empty, being and non-being are treated as two sides of a single movement. The sage subtracts rather than accumulates, returns to simplicity, takes the humble place, helps without claiming credit and steps back once the work is done. In Legge's translation, the sage's three "precious things" are gentleness, economy and "shrinking from taking precedence of others."

Questions to sit with

  1. The book opens by saying the Tao cannot be put into words, then goes on to speak of it for many chapters. How can language point toward something it admits it cannot hold?
  2. Water gives way to everything and still wears through stone. Where has yielding accomplished more than pushing, in public life or in your own?
  3. Almost nothing certain is known about the man said to have written the Tao Te Ching. Does the worth of a teaching depend on knowing who first gave it?
