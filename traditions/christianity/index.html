Tradition · first century CE to today · worldwide

Christianity

A tradition that began with a Jewish teacher in Roman Galilee and now counts more than two billion followers, in churches that worship in very different ways.

At a glance

  • Began Galilee and Judea, under Roman rule, in the first century CE
  • Central figure Jesus of Nazareth, a Jewish teacher executed around 30 CE
  • Scripture the Bible, the Hebrew Bible (the Old Testament, to Christians) joined to the New Testament
  • Followers more than two billion, close to three in ten people on earth

Where it began

It starts with a Jewish teacher in a Roman province. Jesus of Nazareth taught about the kingdom of God, often in parables, until the Roman authorities had him crucified around 30 CE. Historians accept that much; Tacitus and Josephus, writing outside the movement, both mention him.

After that, history hands the story to faith. His followers said God had raised him from the dead. Within a generation, communities met in cities around the eastern Mediterranean, many founded by Paul of Tarsus, whose letters are the earliest surviving Christian texts. For nearly three centuries Christians were a minority, at times persecuted. Under Constantine they won legal toleration in 313, and by the century's end theirs was the empire's official faith.

What Christians believe

Christians believe in one God, and most churches teach that this one God is a Trinity: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. They hold that in Jesus, God became human, a teaching called the incarnation, and that his death and resurrection mend the bond between people and God. That gift is called grace, and its result salvation. Easter, the resurrection feast, crowns the year.

Asked for the heart of the law, Jesus answered with two commands from the Hebrew scriptures: love God with everything you have, and love your neighbor as yourself. Many Christians treat that pair as the faith in a sentence.

How Christians practice

Most Christians meet on Sunday to pray, hear scripture and share bread and wine as the Gospels say Jesus did at his last supper, a rite called Mass, the Eucharist or Communion. Baptism with water brings a person into the church. The calendar follows Jesus's life through Advent, Christmas, Lent, Easter and Pentecost.

One faith, many churches

The tradition has never spoken with one voice. Each branch describes itself in its own terms: - Catholic Christians, about half the total, read scripture alongside church tradition and recognize the pope as the church's visible head. - Orthodox Christians belong to the Eastern Orthodox churches, which parted from Rome in 1054, or to the Oriental Orthodox churches, among them the Coptic, Ethiopian, Armenian and Syriac churches. They keep ancient liturgies and icons. - Protestant churches, from Lutherans and Anglicans to Baptists and Methodists, grew out of the sixteenth-century Reformation and stress the authority of scripture. - Pentecostal Christians emphasize the gifts of the Spirit. Most histories date the movement to a 1906 revival on Azusa Street in Los Angeles, some to Topeka, Kansas, in 1901.

Most Christians now live in the global South.

The contemplative strand

Every branch also holds a quieter line: people who sought God in silence. It runs from Egypt's desert monks and Orthodox monks repeating the Jesus Prayer, through medieval mystics and the Spanish Carmelites Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross, to Quakers in silent meeting. The Cloud of Unknowing, by an unnamed English monk around 1375, is one of its classic guides. Ideas it shares with other traditions include divine love, surrender and nonduality.

Key figures

  • Antony of Egypt (c. 251–356): desert hermit honored by Orthodox and Catholics as a father of monastic life.
  • Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179): Benedictine abbess, visionary, composer and medical writer.
  • Francis of Assisi (1182–1226): renounced his inheritance to live among the poor; founded the Franciscans.
  • Marguerite Porete (d. 1310): Beguine laywoman burned in Paris for The Mirror of Simple Souls.
  • Julian of Norwich (c. 1343–after 1416): English anchoress, whose Short and Long Texts are the earliest surviving writings in English known to be by a woman.
  • Joan of Arc (c. 1412–1431): farm girl who said saints' voices sent her to lead an army.
  • Martin Luther (1483–1546): German monk whose 95 Theses helped spark the Reformation.
  • Harriet Tubman (c. 1822–1913): led enslaved people north, guided by visions she took as God's voice.
  • Howard Thurman (1899–1981): minister and author of Jesus and the Disinherited.
  • Dorothy Day (1897–1980): co-founded the Catholic Worker and its houses of hospitality.
