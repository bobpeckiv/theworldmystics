Buddhism · 1917–2011 · New Jersey to San Diego

Charlotte Joko Beck

A pianist and single mother who began Zen practice in her late forties, she broke with her own teacher and taught that ordinary life, unadorned, is the practice.

At a glance

  • Born New Jersey, March 27, 1917
  • Died June 15, 2011, at ninety-four
  • Tradition Zen Buddhism, trained under Taizan Maezumi, Hakuun Yasutani and Sōen Nakagawa
  • Known for founding the Zen Center of San Diego (1983) and the Ordinary Mind Zen School (1995), and the books Everyday Zen and Nothing Special
  • Emblem the wooden spoon

The life

Charlotte Joko Beck was born in New Jersey on March 27, 1917. She trained as a pianist at the Oberlin Conservatory of Music and worked for some years as a pianist and piano teacher. She married and had four children. According to her friend and fellow teacher Gerry Shishin Wick, the marriage became dangerous, and she left it. She moved to San Diego, took a job as a technical secretary at General Dynamics, and raised the four children on her own.

In 1966, at a Unitarian church in San Diego, she heard a talk by a visiting Japanese Zen priest, Taizan Maezumi. She later said she understood little of it, but she was struck by the way he bowed to each person at the door afterward. She began to sit zazen, seated meditation, with a small group of women, five minutes at a time. She was in her late forties. One member of the group, Shirley Syson, died of cancer, and Wick believed that the peace Beck saw in her friend's dying was the main force behind her practice. She also trained with Hakuun Yasutani and Sōen Nakagawa. For the rest of her life she said plainly that she did not enjoy sitting, or sesshin, the long silent retreats. She kept at both anyway.

For more than a decade she drove two hours each way to the Zen Center of Los Angeles to study with Maezumi, and she later lived there and worked as his secretary. She received Dharma transmission from him (in 1978 by James Ishmael Ford's account; the White Plum Asanga gives 1983), the formal recognition that she could teach in his line. She later broke with him over his personal conduct, which she described to Wick as marked by alcoholism and womanizing, and she turned down his attempts at reconciliation. Few in American Zen were then willing to break publicly with a teacher over misconduct.

In 1983 she founded the Zen Center of San Diego and led it as head teacher until 2006. The two books she published in her lifetime, Everyday Zen: Love and Work (1989) and Nothing Special: Living Zen (1993), found a wide readership for their plain language and psychological directness. She set aside much of the Japanese form, including robes, shaved heads and honorific titles, so that students would not mistake the trappings for the practice. In 1995 she and three senior students founded the Ordinary Mind Zen School, a network of independent centers.

In 2006 she stepped down in San Diego and revoked Dharma transmission from two of her senior successors; the full reasons were never made public. She moved to Prescott, Arizona, taught until she retired in late 2010, and died on June 15, 2011, at ninety-four.

Core teaching

Beck taught that nothing needs to be added to ordinary life to make it spiritual: the life a person already has, examined closely, is the whole of the practice. The Preface to Nothing Special: Living Zen opens the point in two sentences: "Living Zen is nothing special: life as it is. Zen is life itself, nothing added." Her method was to turn directly toward what a person most wants to avoid, usually a plain and unwelcome feeling such as anger, fear or grief, and to stay with it rather than escape it. She treated this material not as a distraction from meditation but as its real subject, and she was careful to say that bearing suffering is not the same as passivity. Several of her successors trained as psychologists and psychiatrists, and her teaching helped bring Zen practice and Western psychology into conversation. Readers who knew her gentle books sometimes expected a gentle teacher; Wick remembered her instead as exacting, "Joshu's unsheathed sword."

In her own words

"Until we bow down and bear the suffering of life—not opposing it, but absorbing it and being it—we cannot see what our life is." —Charlotte Joko Beck, "To Totally Be Under," adapted from Everyday Zen: Love and Work, in Tricycle: The Buddhist Review

The essay builds on the root of the word "suffering," from the Latin for bearing something from beneath. Most people, she writes, meet trouble as something pressing down on them from outside, to be fought off. Her alternative is not resignation but a change of position: supporting the weight from underneath instead of being crushed by it.

Questions to sit with

  1. Beck began meditating in her late forties, after years of raising four children on her own. What might a late start bring to a serious undertaking that an early one cannot?
  2. She broke with the teacher who had authorized her to teach, because of how he lived. When should respect for a teacher or an institution give way to judgment about conduct?
  3. She taught that anger, fear and grief are the material of practice rather than obstacles to it. What changes when a difficult feeling is treated as something to study instead of something to get rid of?
