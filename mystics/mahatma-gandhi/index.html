Mahatma Gandhi

A young lawyer forced off a South African train learned to resist without striking back, and used that method to help lead India to independence.

“The means may be likened to a seed, the end to a tree; and there is just the same inviolable connection between the means and the end as there is between the seed and the tree.”

M. K. Gandhi, Indian Home Rule (Hind Swaraj), ch. XVI, M. K. Gandhi, 1922

At a glance

  • Born Porbandar, Gujarat, India, October 2, 1869
  • Died New Delhi, India, January 30, 1948
  • Tradition Hinduism, shaped by the Jain teaching of nonviolence
  • Known for satyagraha, the method of nonviolent mass resistance, and the movement for India's independence
  • Emblem the sandals

The life

Mohandas Gandhi was born on October 2, 1869, in Porbandar, on India's western coast, where his father served as chief minister to the ruler of a small princely state. His mother prayed and fasted often, and Jain monks, who teach that no living thing should be harmed, were regular visitors. He studied law in London, froze during his first case in a Bombay courtroom, and in 1893 took a job in South Africa. Soon after he arrived, he was put off a train at Pietermaritzburg for refusing to leave the first-class compartment he had a ticket for after a white passenger objected, and he chose to stay and fight the prejudice behind it.

South Africa became his workshop for twenty-one years. On an overnight train he read John Ruskin's Unto This Last, a book about the dignity of ordinary work, and decided to change his life by morning. He started a farm where everyone did the same labor. He wrote to the Russian novelist Leo Tolstoy and named a second settlement Tolstoy Farm. In 1906, when a new law required Indians to be fingerprinted and registered, he stood before three thousand people in a Johannesburg theatre, and they swore together to disobey it and accept the punishment.

He came home to India in 1915. His political mentor, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, told him to spend a year traveling the country and listening before he spoke. Within two years he was standing with indigo farmers in Champaran, and the next year with mill workers in Ahmedabad. In 1922, after a crowd at Chauri Chaura killed police officers during a protest movement he had called for, he halted the campaign and fasted. Charged with sedition, he pleaded guilty and asked the judge for the heaviest penalty the law allowed. He was sentenced to six years.

Every day he spun his own thread on a wheel, as India's village poor did, and he wore the plain hand-spun cloth it produced. The wheel became a sign of self-reliance across the country. He opened his prayer meetings with readings from the Bhagavad Gita, the Quran, and the Gospels. In 1931 he went to Buckingham Palace in a loincloth, and when he was asked whether he had worn enough to meet the King, he is said to have answered that the King had on enough for both of them.

In August 1947 India won its independence and was divided into two countries, India and Pakistan, and violence between Hindus and Muslims spread. Gandhi did not celebrate in Delhi. He went to Calcutta and fasted until the killing there stopped. On January 30, 1948, as he walked to evening prayer in New Delhi, he was shot and killed. He was seventy-eight.

Core teaching

Gandhi described the aim of his life as self-realization, which he also called seeing God face to face. He came to identify that aim with Truth, and he deliberately reversed the familiar formula: rather than "God is Truth," he said "Truth is God," a form even a nonbeliever could accept. If Truth is the goal, nonviolence (ahimsa) is the way to it, and he treated the two as inseparable, since harming another assumes a separateness he denied; he professed belief in advaita, the essential unity of all that lives. From this came satyagraha, "holding fast to truth": resistance that tries to persuade an opponent rather than defeat him, and that accepts suffering, through fasting, jail or blows, instead of inflicting it. He held that God is met most surely in service to the poorest, and he treated daily labor such as spinning as a form of prayer.

Questions to sit with

  1. Gandhi pleaded guilty and asked for the heaviest sentence the law allowed. Why would someone who thinks a law is unjust accept its punishment? Does that make a protest stronger or weaker?
  2. Gandhi compared means and ends to a seed and a tree. Can a good goal ever justify an unfair way of reaching it? Where have you seen that tested, in history or in your own life?
  3. After the violence at Chauri Chaura, Gandhi stopped a campaign at the height of its strength. How responsible is a leader for what followers do in the leader's name?
