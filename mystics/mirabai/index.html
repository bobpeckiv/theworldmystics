Mirabai

A Rajput princess of sixteenth-century Rajasthan, she refused the life laid out for a royal widow and spent her days singing to Krishna, whom she called her husband.

At a glance

  • Born around 1498 by the traditional dating, in Kudki, in present-day Rajasthan, into the Mertia branch of the Rathore Rajputs
  • Died around 1546, by tradition at Dwarka on the Gujarat coast; the exact dates are debated
  • Tradition Hinduism, in the bhakti movement, as a devotee of Krishna
  • Known for devotional songs (bhajans) in Rajasthani and Braj that are still sung across India
  • Emblem the ektara, the one-stringed instrument of wandering singers

The life

Her life comes down through song and through devotional biographies written after her death, chiefly Nabhadas's Bhaktamal of about 1600 and Priyadas's commentary on it of 1712. Historians accept a core of events and treat much of the rest as the tradition's portrait of her.

By the traditional reckoning she was born around 1498 at Kudki, in what is now Rajasthan. Her father, Ratan Singh, was a minor noble of the Mertia Rathores. Her grandfather Rao Dudaji ruled Merta and was a devotee of Vishnu who kept an image of Krishna. Tradition says her mother died when she was small and that she grew up in her grandfather's house, close to that image. A well-loved childhood story tells that, seeing a bridegroom's procession go by, she asked who her own groom would be and was told, half in jest, that it was Krishna. She is said to have held to that answer for the rest of her life.

Around 1516 she was married to Bhoj Raj, heir to the throne of Mewar and son of Rana Sanga, one of the strongest Rajput rulers of the day. She took her Krishna image to the Mewar capital at Chittor, and the stories say she declined to worship the goddess honored by her husband's family. Bhoj Raj died around 1521, probably of wounds from battle. Her father fell at Khanwa in 1527, where the Rajput confederacy was defeated by Babur, founder of the Mughal dynasty, and Rana Sanga died the next year.

A royal widow was expected either to die on her husband's pyre, the custom called sati, or to live hidden away in mourning. According to the tradition she did neither. She said her husband was Krishna, and that he was alive. She passed her days at the temple, singing in Rajasthani and Braj rather than Sanskrit, dancing before the image with bells at her ankles and receiving wandering holy men of every caste. Tradition names as her guru Ravidas, a poet-saint from a leatherworking caste; scholars debate whether the two ever met. The biographies say that her brother-in-law Vikramaditya, once he ruled Mewar, tried to have her killed, and that none of the attempts harmed her.

Some time between about 1533 and 1538 she left Mewar for the pilgrim roads. She is said to have gone to Merta, then to Vrindavan and Mathura, the land of Krishna's youth, and at last to Dwarka, the city of Krishna as king. Tradition holds that she died there around 1546, merging into the image of Krishna in the temple; historians make no claim about how she died.

Hundreds of songs carry her name. Only two appear in manuscripts from before the early eighteenth century, and scholars such as John Stratton Hawley caution that no single poem can be tied securely to the historical Mirabai. The songs are still sung in temples, homes and gatherings across South Asia and its diaspora, and she is among the most widely loved women saints in Hinduism.

Core teaching

Mirabai belongs to the bhakti movement, which held that God can be reached through personal love rather than through ritual, learning or rank. Her songs address Krishna as a beloved in a personal form, the saguna way of devotion, and they treat that love as the only qualification a seeker needs: not birth, caste, sex, priesthood or a husband's permission. Because they were sung in the spoken languages of the region, anyone could take them up, and they traveled far beyond the court and the temple.

At its height, devotion of this kind is called parabhakti, the highest love, in which the line between the one who loves and the one who is loved gives way. Where other paths approach that union through inquiry or meditation, the songs credited to her approach it through longing, music and the body: singing, dancing and giving up everything else. The tradition reads her refusals of the roles offered to her, as wife, widow and princess, as the outward form of the same surrender. The songs often carry it with humor and delight rather than grief.

A story told about her

The best-known story, kept in the devotional biographies, concerns a cup of poison. Vikramaditya, the tale goes, had it carried to her by a messenger who presented it as a kindly gift. She offered the drink to Krishna, as she offered everything she took, then drank it, and it did her no harm. Other episodes follow the same pattern: a basket of flowers sent as an offering with a cobra coiled inside, which turned to a garland in her hands, and a bed of nails. Historians treat these episodes as legend, set down long after her death. The tradition keeps them because they say what it believed about her: that a person who has already given everything away has nothing left that can be taken.

Questions to sit with

  1. Mirabai answered every role her society offered her by saying she already belonged to someone else. When does loyalty to something unseen set a person free, and when might it become a way of avoiding the people in front of them?
  2. She sang in the languages ordinary people spoke rather than the language of the priests. What changes when sacred things are said in everyday words?
  3. Almost none of the songs that carry her name can be traced securely to her. Does a song stop being hers once generations of singers have shaped it?
