Rumi

A respected jurist and preacher in thirteenth-century Konya, Rumi was remade by his friendship with a wandering dervish and became one of the most widely read poets of Sufism.

“Listen to this reed how it complains: it is telling a tale of separations.”

Rumi, Masnavi, Book I, line 1, Reynold A. Nicholson, 1926

At a glance

  • Born 1207, traditionally in Balkh, in present-day Afghanistan
  • Died December 17, 1273, in Konya, in present-day Turkey
  • Tradition Islam, in its Sufi stream; the Mevlevi order formed around his memory after his death
  • Known for the Masnavi and the Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi
  • Emblem the whirling sun

The life

Jalal al-Din Muhammad, called Mawlana, "our master," by his followers and Rumi in the West, a name meaning "from Rum," the old term for Anatolia, was born in 1207 at the eastern edge of the Persian-speaking world, traditionally in the city of Balkh. His father, Baha al-Din Walad, was a preacher, jurist and Sufi of wide reputation.

While Rumi was still a boy the family left Balkh. The sources give different reasons for the move, and within a few years Mongol armies had swept through the region. The family traveled west in stages, by way of Nishapur and Baghdad, on pilgrimage to Mecca and into Syria. Around 1228, at the invitation of the Seljuk sultan, they settled in Konya in central Anatolia, a city where Muslims, Christians and Jews, Persians, Greeks, Turks and Armenians lived side by side.

When his father died in 1231, Rumi took over his teaching post. For more than a decade he was one of Konya's leading religious scholars, teaching law, preaching to large gatherings and reading the Persian Sufi poets Sanai and Attar.

In the autumn of 1244 he met Shams of Tabriz, a wandering dervish many years his senior, with no school and almost no possessions. Accounts of their first exchange vary, but all agree the meeting overturned Rumi's life. The two withdrew together for long stretches; tradition speaks of forty days. Rumi set aside his lecturing, began to compose poetry and took up the music and turning dance later known as the sema. His students resented the stranger, and in 1246 Shams left for Damascus. Rumi sent his son to bring him back. The resentment returned, and around 1247–48 Shams was gone. What happened is uncertain: Sultan Walad, Rumi's son and the earliest witness, records only a disappearance, while chronicles written later accuse Rumi's disciples of killing him.

Grief turned into poetry. The Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi, more than thirty thousand verses of lyric poetry addressed to the lost friend, came out of the years that followed. Over roughly the last fifteen years of his life Rumi dictated the Masnavi-ye Ma'navi, some twenty-five thousand couplets in six books, to his disciple Husam al-Din Chalabi. Its blend of stories, jokes, Qur'anic reflection and teaching led later readers to call it "the Persian Qur'an." His talks were collected as Fihi ma Fihi.

Rumi died in Konya on December 17, 1273. In a story told by the Mevlevi chronicler Aflaki, Jews and Christians joined the funeral procession, saying Rumi had deepened their understanding of their own holy books. The Mevlevi order, known in the West for its "whirling dervishes," took shape around his memory after his death. His tomb in Konya still draws visitors of many faiths, and in translation he has become one of the most widely read poets in the world.

Core teaching

At the center of Rumi's teaching is love, ishq, understood not as one feeling among others but as the pull that draws every created thing back toward God, the Beloved. The Masnavi opens with a reed flute cut from its reed bed. Its music is a lament, and its longing to return is the longing of every soul parted from its source. For Rumi that ache is not a fault to be cured but the road itself. In the Sufi language he inherited, the goal is fana, the passing away of the separate self in God, and baqa, the life that continues in God afterward. Lover and Beloved prove in the end not to be two. Rumi held that this could not stay a theory. It had to be lived through the body, in music and movement as much as in study. His poems range freely across Qur'anic allusion, folk tale, comedy and prayer, and they open a wide door: in one passage of the Masnavi, the religion of love is set apart from every other religion.

Questions to sit with

  1. Rumi treats longing as the path rather than a problem to be solved. What would change if an ache you carry were read as a direction instead of a defect?
  2. A respected scholar let one meeting with a stranger his students distrusted overturn his settled life. When is it wise to let a single encounter change everything, and when is it reckless?
  3. According to one story, people of several faiths mourned him as their own. What makes a teacher belong to more than one tradition?
