Guru Tegh Bahadur

The ninth Sikh Guru, Tegh Bahadur wrote spare verses on the passing world and was put to death in Delhi in 1675; Sikhs honor him as a martyr for conscience.

“Saith Nanak, hear, O man, call him a person possessed of divine knowledge, / Who inspireth no fear, and who hath no fear of others.”

Guru Tegh Bahadur, Salok 16, Guru Granth Sahib, Max Arthur Macauliffe, 1909

At a glance

  • Born 1621, at Amritsar, in the Punjab
  • Died November 1675, at Chandni Chowk in Delhi, by execution
  • Tradition Sikhism, as its ninth Guru
  • Known for 116 compositions in the Guru Granth Sahib, among them the saloks near its close; founding the town later called Anandpur
  • Emblem bubble on water

The life

Tegh Bahadur was born at Amritsar in 1621 to Mata Nanaki and the sixth Guru, Hargobind, the last of their sons. He was first called Tyag Mal, a name that speaks of renunciation. It was a household changed by the death of Guru Arjan, Hargobind's father, in Mughal custody: Hargobind kept armed followers and fought several clashes with Mughal forces. The boy was trained in riding and swordsmanship as well as scripture, and tradition names Bhai Gurdas and Baba Buddha, the scribe and the first reader of the Adi Granth, among his teachers. After he fought at the battle of Kartarpur in 1635, at about fourteen, he became known as Tegh Bahadur, "brave of the sword."

He married Mata Gujri, and the family later settled at Bakala, a village near the Beas. There he lived for a long period in devotion and retirement, largely out of public view. When Hargobind died in 1644 the leadership went to a grandson, Har Rai, and then to Har Rai's young son, Har Krishan. Tegh Bahadur contested neither succession.

In 1664 Har Krishan, still a child, died of smallpox in Delhi. As tradition reports his last words, they pointed to "Baba Bakale," the elder at Bakala, and several men there put themselves forward as that elder. The best-known story, of a merchant named Makhan Shah who tested the claimants and found the true Guru, is tradition rather than record, but that same year Tegh Bahadur was acknowledged as the ninth Guru.

Much of the following decade was spent on the road. In 1665 he founded Chak Nanaki, the settlement later known as Anandpur. He then traveled east through Agra, Allahabad, Banaras and Patna into Bengal and Assam, establishing congregations and having wells dug, and Sikh sources credit him with helping make peace between the Ahom kingdom of Assam and a Mughal army under Ram Singh. His only son, Gobind Rai, the future Guru Gobind Singh, was born at Patna in 1666 while his father was away.

The empire was then ruled by Aurangzeb. In 1675 Tegh Bahadur was arrested and taken to Delhi. Accounts of what led to the arrest differ, and historians have not settled the question. The Sikh account, whose earliest form is a passage in the Bachittar Natak, a work attributed to his son, holds that he gave his life to protect the religious freedom of others. Tradition says that Brahmins from Kashmir had come to him seeking help against forced conversion, although the Sikh tradition had long refused the caste order that placed Brahmins at its head. Sikh accounts add that three companions held with him, Bhai Mati Das, Bhai Sati Das and Bhai Dyala, were put to death first. He was beheaded at Chandni Chowk, Delhi's great market street, in November 1675; sources differ on the day. His son became the tenth Guru, and his 116 hymns and verses were afterward added to the scripture, his saloks standing close to its end.

Core teaching

Guru Tegh Bahadur's verses are plain, brief and insistent. Many are addressed not to a listener but to the poet's own mind, which he calls back, again and again, from what it clings to. Their central theme is impermanence: wealth, family, youth and the body all pass, and the world is likened to a dream, or to a bubble that forms on water and is gone. Only the divine Name endures, and remembering it is the way through. The obstacle is haumai, the ego that claims ownership and sorts experience into gain and loss. A person freed from it is known by equanimity. In Max Arthur Macauliffe's 1909 translation, such a person is one "Who feeleth neither joy nor sorrow, and who treateth an enemy and a friend as the same." That freedom is not postponed to an afterlife. It shows in conduct, and above all in fearlessness that runs both ways, toward the fear one feels and the fear one causes.

A story told about him

After the execution, the story goes, the Guru's followers recovered his remains in defiance of the authorities. Bhai Jaita is said to have carried the head north to Anandpur, to the Guru's young son. Lakhi Shah Vanjara, a carrier who ran carts for a living, is said to have taken the body to his own house at Raisina and set the house on fire, so that the Guru could be cremated without drawing notice. The details come from Sikh tradition rather than from records of the time, but two Delhi gurdwaras keep the memory: Sis Ganj, where the Guru was beheaded, and Rakab Ganj, where the house is said to have stood.

Questions to sit with

  1. Tegh Bahadur spent long years out of public view before he took up leadership. What might a stretch of obscurity make possible that constant visibility does not?
  2. The Sikh tradition remembers him as dying for the freedom of people whose faith was not his own. Whose freedom would you defend even where you disagree with how they would use it?
  3. He described the wise as those who neither frighten others nor are frightened by them. Where in your own life do fearing and frightening turn out to be connected?
