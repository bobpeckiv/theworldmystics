Francis of Assisi

The son of a rich cloth merchant, Francis of Assisi gave away everything he owned, founded the Franciscan friars and sang of sun, moon and death as kin.

“My little sisters the birds, ye owe much to God, your Creator, and ye ought to sing his praise at all times and in all places, because he has given you liberty to fly about into all places”

Francis of Assisi, preaching to the birds, as told in The Little Flowers of St Francis, ch. XVI, the first English translation (1864), credited to Lady Georgiana Fullerton, as revised by Dom Roger Hudleston, 1926

At a glance

  • Born 1181 or 1182, in Assisi, in the Umbrian hills of central Italy
  • Died 3 October 1226, at the Porziuncola, below Assisi
  • Tradition Christianity, as founder of the Franciscan friars
  • Known for voluntary poverty; the Canticle of Brother Sun; the stories gathered in the Little Flowers
  • Emblem the knotted cord

The life

Francis was born in Assisi in 1181 or 1182. He was baptized Giovanni, but his father, Pietro di Bernardone, a prosperous cloth merchant who traded in France, called him Francesco, "the Frenchman," and the name stayed. His early biographers describe a lively, free-spending young man who loved songs and company and hoped to become a knight. In about 1202 he fought for Assisi against neighboring Perugia, was captured, and spent about a year as a prisoner before his father paid the ransom. He came home ill.

A second attempt at soldiering ended on the road toward Apulia. Bonaventure's later Life says a voice in the night asked why he was leaving the Lord to serve a servant, and he turned back. In his Testament, dictated near the end of his life, Francis traced his conversion to time spent among lepers, whom he had once found unbearable to look at; what had seemed bitter, he wrote, became sweet. Praying in the half-ruined chapel of San Damiano, below the town, he heard, by his biographers' account, the crucifix tell him to repair God's house. He took the words literally and sold cloth from his father's shop to pay for the work. His furious father brought him before the bishop, and there, in about 1206, Francis returned the money and even the clothes on his back, renouncing his inheritance.

He lived as a beggar and mender of chapels until others joined him, the first being Bernard of Quintavalle. In 1209 or 1210 Pope Innocent III gave spoken approval to their plain rule of life, drawn from the Gospels, and Francis named the group the Friars Minor, the lesser brothers. In 1212 Clare of Assisi left her family to follow the same path, and the women who gathered around her became the Poor Clares. In 1219, during the Fifth Crusade, he crossed the lines at Damietta in Egypt and met the sultan al-Kamil, who received him and let him go. As the movement outgrew him, Pope Honorius III approved a revised Rule in 1223.

In September 1224, on the mountain of La Verna, he saw, as the sources tell it, a crucified figure with six wings, and afterward bore wounds in his hands, feet and side like those of the crucified Christ. His companions and Thomas of Celano's Life, written within three years of his death, describe these stigmata. Nearly blind and in pain, he composed the Canticle of Brother Sun, among the earliest surviving poems in Italian, praising God through Brother Sun, Sister Moon, wind, water and fire. Near death he added a verse welcoming Sister Bodily Death. He died at the Porziuncola on 3 October 1226 and was declared a saint in 1228.

About a century later, friars of his order wrote down the stories that became the Little Flowers, among them the sermon to the birds. The prayer that begins "Lord, make me an instrument of your peace" is not his; it was first printed anonymously in 1912.

Core teaching

Francis wrote no systematic theology. His teaching was a way of life in which poverty, humility and peace were taken literally. He asked his brothers to own nothing, not as hardship but as freedom from the possessiveness that sets one person above another, and he called them minores, the lesser ones, so that they would stand below everyone they served. In the Admonitions he wrote, in the translation of Regis Armstrong, Wayne Hellmann and William Short in Francis of Assisi: Early Documents, "What a person is before God, that he is and no more." Rank, wealth and reputation add nothing to that. His sense of kinship reached beyond humanity: in the Canticle of Brother Sun, sun, moon, wind, water, fire and earth are brothers and sisters, and at the last so is death. The same refusal to stand apart shaped his conduct toward lepers, whom his society shut outside its walls, and toward Muslims; his Rule of 1221 told friars living among them to avoid quarrels and disputes. Preaching, he held, should be done first by works.

A story told about him

The Little Flowers tells of a wolf that preyed on the town of Gubbio, killing animals and people until no one dared go outside the walls. Against everyone's advice, Francis went out to meet it. As the story goes, the wolf ran at him with open jaws; he made the sign of the cross, called it brother, and it lay down at his feet. He told it he knew hunger had driven it to kill and offered terms: the townspeople would feed it for the rest of its life, and it would harm no one. The wolf, the book says, set its paw in his hand to seal the pact, then lived for two years in Gubbio, going from house to house to be fed, and the town grieved when it died of old age. The tale cannot be checked, but it has lasted for centuries because its point is plain: peace began when someone walked out to meet the creature everyone feared.

Questions to sit with

  1. Francis said that what had seemed bitter to him turned sweet once he stayed among the people he dreaded. Who or what do you keep at a distance, and what might change if you came closer?
  2. He spoke of owning nothing as a kind of freedom. Which possession, role or reputation would be hardest for you to hand back, and what is it protecting?
  3. The Canticle calls the sun, water and even death brother and sister. How would an ordinary day change if the things around you were treated as kin rather than as resources?
