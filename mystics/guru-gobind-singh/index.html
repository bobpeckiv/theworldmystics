Sikhism · 1666–1708 · Patna to Nanded

Guru Gobind Singh

Tenth and last of the human Sikh Gurus, Gobind Singh created the Khalsa in 1699 and handed the Guruship on to the scripture and the Khalsa Panth.

“The temple and the mosque are the same; the Hindu worship and the Musalman prayer are the same; all men are the same; it is through error they appear different.”

Guru Gobind Singh, Akal Ustat, Max Arthur Macauliffe, 1909

At a glance

  • Born 1666, in Patna, Bihar, in eastern India
  • Died October 1708, at Nanded, on the Godavari River in the Deccan
  • Tradition Sikhism, as the tenth and last of its human Gurus
  • Known for founding the Khalsa in 1699; the writings gathered in the Dasam Granth; passing the Guruship on to scripture and community, the Guru Granth Sahib and Khalsa Panth
  • Emblem the iron bowl

The life

Gobind Rai was born at Patna, on the Ganges, in 1666; his father, Tegh Bahadur, ninth of the Sikh Gurus, was then traveling in the east. As a child he was brought west to Anandpur, the town his father had founded in the Shivalik foothills of the Punjab. In 1675 Tegh Bahadur was put to death in Delhi by the Mughal government of the emperor Aurangzeb. In the Sikh account he had gone to plead for Kashmiri Brahmins facing forced conversion; some historians read the sources differently, and the cause is still debated. His son, then nine, became the tenth Guru.

His court at Anandpur drew poets and scholars working in Braj, Persian, Sanskrit and Punjabi, and wrote a large body of verse himself. He also gathered an armed following. This alarmed the Hindu rajas of the nearby hill states, and his first battle, at Bhangani in 1688, was fought against a coalition of them rather than against the Mughals.

In 1699, at the spring festival of Vaisakhi, he founded the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs. As Sikh tradition tells it, he faced a great gathering, sword drawn, and called for a man ready to give his head. Five volunteers, from different regions and castes, went with him one at a time into a tent and afterward reappeared alive. He initiated them with amrit, sweetened water stirred with a khanda, a double-edged sword, while hymns were recited, then knelt to receive the same rite from them. Khalsa men took the name Singh, "lion," and, in the tradition's account, the women took Kaur. Initiated Sikhs keep the Five Ks: uncut hair (kesh), a wooden comb (kangha), a steel bracelet (kara), a cotton undergarment (kachera) and a sword (kirpan). Sikh tradition traces the rule to 1699; some historians, W. H. McLeod among them, hold that the list took its settled form in later codes of conduct.

In 1704 Mughal troops and allied hill rajas besieged Anandpur. Its defenders left under a promise of safe passage and were attacked on the road. His elder sons, Ajit Singh and Jujhar Singh, were killed in the fighting at Chamkaur. The younger two, Zorawar Singh and Fateh Singh, roughly nine and six, were executed at Sirhind on the orders of its governor, Wazir Khan, and their grandmother, Mata Gujri, died in custody there. Sikh tradition holds that the boys refused to give up their faith. In 1705 the Guru sent Aurangzeb the Zafarnama, a letter in Persian verse charging the emperor's officers with breaking their oaths.

After Aurangzeb died in 1707, Gobind Singh met the new emperor, Bahadur Shah, and traveled south with his camp. At Nanded he was stabbed by an assassin, and accounts differ over who sent the man. The wound proved fatal in October 1708. In the Sikh account, he declared before his death that no human Guru would follow him: authority would rest with the scripture and with the Khalsa Panth. The words are first recorded in 1769.

Core teaching

Guru Gobind Singh taught within the Sikh understanding of one God, whom he praised in the Jaap Sahib and Akal Ustat as Akal Purakh, the Timeless Being: formless, unborn, beyond every caste and outward sign, and not reached through priests, images or rites. Sikh thought holds this God to be Creator, present in creation yet distinct from the soul that worships, so his devotion is theistic rather than a doctrine that self and God are identical. Its practice, as for Guru Nanak, is naam simran, steady remembrance of the divine Name. What he added was a form of life. The Khalsa ideal of the sant-sipahi, the saint-soldier, joins prayer and readiness to defend others in one person, carrying forward miri-piri, the joining of temporal and spiritual authority associated with his grandfather, Guru Hargobind. One bowl and one shared name set caste aside inside the community, and by vesting the Guruship in a book and a people he made authority collective. The Dasam Granth, the collection of writings under his name, was compiled after his death. The Jaap Sahib, Akal Ustat, Bachittar Natak and Zafarnama are widely accepted as his; the authorship of other parts is debated among Sikh scholars.

A story told about him

A sakhi, a traditional Sikh story, tells of Bhai Kanhaiya, a follower who carried water to the wounded during the fighting around Anandpur. He gave it to every fallen man he reached, Sikh or Mughal soldier alike. Some Sikhs complained to the Guru that he was reviving the enemy. Asked to explain himself, Kanhaiya is said to have answered that on the field he saw no friend and no foe, only the same divine presence in every face. The Guru, the story goes, approved and gave him ointment so that he could dress wounds as well. No record from the period confirms the episode, but it is often told to show what service meant in a community that also carried swords.

Questions to sit with

  1. He knelt to receive initiation from the five people he had just initiated. What would it mean for someone who holds authority, perhaps yourself, to stand inside a rule rather than above it?
  2. The Khalsa joins prayer with readiness to defend others. Where do you keep contemplation and action apart, and what is gained or lost by keeping them apart?
  3. At the end he left authority to a scripture and a gathered people rather than to a successor. Which do you find easier to trust, a single person or a shared text and a people, and why?
