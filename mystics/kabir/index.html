Kabir

A weaver of fifteenth-century Benares who probably never learned to write, Kabir mocked priests, caste and ritual in songs still sung by Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs.

“The river and its waves are one surf: where is the difference between the river and its waves? When the wave rises, it is the water; and when it falls, it is the same water again. Tell me, Sir, where is the distinction?”

Kabir, Songs of Kabir, no. XIV (Sen II. 56), Rabindranath Tagore, 1915

At a glance

  • Born around 1440 by the traditional dating, in Benares (Varanasi), into a family of Muslim weavers; some historians place his life earlier, about 1398–1448
  • Died around 1518 by tradition, at Maghar, in present-day Uttar Pradesh
  • Tradition the bhakti movement of North India, as one of the Sants; his verses also stand in the Sikh scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib
  • Known for couplets and songs in the spoken Hindi of his region, gathered in the Bijak, the Kabir Granthavali and the Guru Granth Sahib
  • Emblem the river steps

The life

Few facts about Kabir are secure. Scholars accept a small core and treat the rest as the tradition's portrait of him.

By the traditional dating he lived from about 1440 to about 1518. He belonged to the julahas of Benares, now Varanasi, a community of weavers whose families had only lately converted to Islam. That set him low in two social orders at once: high-caste Hindus counted weavers as polluting, and Muslims of better birth looked down on them. He almost certainly never learned to read. He composed aloud, worked at the loom throughout his life and lived as a householder; tradition names his wife as Loi.

Legend fills what the record leaves blank. One story makes him the abandoned child of a Brahmin widow, found beside the Lahara Tara pond and raised by a childless Muslim couple, Niru and Nima. Another tells how he sought out Ramananda, the great Vaishnava teacher of the city, who would not take a Muslim pupil. Kabir is said to have lain before dawn on the bathing steps of the Ganges where Ramananda came down to the river; the teacher trod on him, cried "Ram! Ram!" in surprise, and Kabir claimed the name as his initiation. Scholars following Charlotte Vaudeville doubt the meeting, since the two men's dates may not overlap.

His verse took three main forms: dohas, or couplets, sakhis, "witness" verses, and pads, songs. He used the mixed vernacular of the region rather than the Sanskrit of the pandits or the Arabic of the mosque, and he turned it on both establishments: on image worship and the pilgrim's bath, on ritual prayer and the journey to Mecca, on caste and the authority of books. He claimed no religion.

Tradition says that late in life he was summoned before Sultan Sikandar Lodi on a charge of heresy, survived miraculous attempts on his life and was banished from Benares. Many scholars doubt the persecution stories altogether. His last years are placed at Maghar, a town popular belief held to be an unlucky place to die, while dying in Benares was thought to bring liberation. He is said to have chosen it deliberately.

At his death, the best-known story goes, his Hindu followers meant to cremate him and his Muslim followers meant to bury him; when the shroud was lifted, only flowers lay beneath it, and each side took a share.

His verses passed into three collections that overlap but differ: the Bijak of the Kabir Panth, the Kabir Granthavali of the Dadu Panth and the Guru Granth Sahib, where no one outside the Sikh Gurus has more verses. Scholars such as Linda Hess and John Stratton Hawley treat the corpus as a living tradition, not the settled works of one author. The best-known English versions, Rabindranath Tagore's Songs of Kabir (1915), were made from a later collection gathered by Kshitimohan Sen from manuscripts and wandering singers, and some scholars think only a handful of those songs are Kabir's own.

Core teaching

Kabir's teaching turns on one claim: God is not held in any building, rite or book, and is nearer than the seeker supposes. He is counted among the Sants of North India, bhakti poets who approached the divine as nirguna, without form, rather than as an image to be served. From there he attacked whatever set itself between a person and God: priest and mullah, temple and mosque, pilgrimage, fasting, caste and book learning. The attack was the reverse side of an affirmation. If the divine is present in every breath and every living thing, no tradition owns it, and a weaver at work stands as close to it as anyone at an altar. Kabir also pressed for urgency. Freedom was to be found in this life, not deferred until after death. Later readers place his realization within sahaja, the "natural" or spontaneous state that the Sant lineage valued above trance and elaborate practice.

Questions to sit with

  1. Kabir located the sacred in the breath of an ordinary working day rather than in a shrine. What would change if daily work were treated as the place where it is met?
  2. He belonged fully to neither community around him, and both later claimed him. Can standing outside every group be its own kind of belonging?
  3. He told his listeners not to wait for death to be free. What is being put off until later that could be faced now?
