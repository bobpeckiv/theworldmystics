Christianity · c. 1412–1431 · Domrémy to Rouen

Joan of Arc

A French farm girl who could not read, rode with an army at seventeen, and answered a church court so plainly that her words are read six centuries later.

“You say you are my judge. Take care what you are doing; for in truth I am sent by God, and you place yourself in great danger.”

Joan of Arc, to Bishop Pierre Cauchon, Trial of Condemnation, Rouen, February 24, 1431, T. Douglas Murray, 1902

At a glance

  • Born Domrémy, on the Meuse River in eastern France, around 1412
  • Died Rouen, Normandy, May 30, 1431
  • Tradition Christianity, in the Catholic faith of late medieval France
  • Known for lifting the siege of Orléans, seeing Charles VII crowned at Reims, and the trial record of her own answers
  • Emblem the banner

The life

Joan was the daughter of Jacques d'Arc and Isabelle Romée, farmers in Domrémy, a village on the Meuse River, and she was born there around 1412. She never learned to read or write. Neighbors who had known her as a child described an ordinary, devout village girl who was good with a needle and looked after the animals. Her country was deep in the Hundred Years' War with England: England's king said the French throne was his, the Burgundians backed his claim, and Charles, the French heir, still had no crown.

At about thirteen she began to hear voices, which she came to identify as Saint Michael, Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret. In time she believed they had given her a task: to break the English siege of Orléans and see Charles crowned at Reims, where French kings were crowned. In early 1429 she reached his court at Chinon. Witnesses said Charles had hidden himself among his courtiers and that she picked him out of the crowd. Before he would trust her, churchmen at Poitiers examined her for three weeks and found nothing against her.

She was given armor and a household and rode to Orléans, and the siege broke within days. On July 17, 1429, Charles received his crown at Reims, and Joan stood beside the altar holding her banner. She said afterward that it had borne the burden and deserved the honor. Then the war turned. An attack on Paris failed, and in May 1430 Burgundian soldiers captured her at Compiègne, and the English bought her from them.

Her trial at Rouen opened in January 1431, before Bishop Pierre Cauchon and some sixty assessors, most of them university men, all in English pay or within English reach. On May 24, in the cemetery of Saint-Ouen, with a fire already built, she was told she would live if she signed, and she signed a short paper she could not read. Four days later she was wearing men's clothes again, as she had since the ride to Chinon, and she told her judges she had recanted only from fear of the fire. The court declared her relapsed. On May 30 she was burned in the Old Market.

Notaries had taken down her answers in French as she gave them. One of them, Guillaume Manchon, later testified that he had been pressed to change the record and refused. A quarter century after her death, a new inquiry heard sworn testimony from about a hundred and fifteen people, from her childhood neighbors to those same notaries, and in 1456 the verdict was annulled. The Catholic Church declared her a saint in 1920.

Core teaching

Joan left no writings and taught no method. What survives is the way she answered under questioning, taken down by the court's own notaries. Asked if she knew herself to be in a state of grace, a question built so that either yes or no could be used against her, she answered with a prayer covering both cases instead of a claim about herself. Asked whether God hated the English, she said she knew nothing of God's love or hatred for them, only that they would be driven out of France. Across the record she keeps a clean line between what she said she had been told and what she would not claim to know. She treated what her voices told her as coming before every other duty, and she would not deny them on the order of any man.

A story told about her

After her capture, the Burgundians held Joan in a tower at Beaurevoir while they settled her price with the English. Word reached her that Compiègne was about to fall and its people might be killed, and that she herself was being sold. Her voices, she said, told her not to jump. She jumped anyway, from about seventy feet, and was found unconscious; for two or three days she could not eat or drink. At her trial she explained why she had jumped but did not defend it. She called the leap a fault, said she had confessed it and been forgiven, and let a court that wanted her dead hear her admit it.

Questions to sit with

  1. The court treated what Joan wore as one of the gravest charges against her. Why might clothing matter so much to the people who hold power?
  2. Joan signed a paper under threat of the fire and took it back four days later. Does a promise made out of fear count as a promise?
  3. The notary Guillaume Manchon refused to change the record of her answers. What is owed to the truth when telling it plainly comes at a cost?
