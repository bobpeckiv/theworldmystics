Taoism · c. 369–c. 286 BCE · state of Song, China

Zhuangzi

A Warring States thinker from the small state of Song, Zhuangzi taught through jokes, paradoxes and parables, and the book that bears his name became a founding text of Taoism.

At a glance

  • Born around 369 BCE, by the usual estimate, in Meng, a town in the state of Song
  • Died around 286 BCE
  • Tradition Taoism; the Zhuangzi and the Tao Te Ching are its two founding texts
  • Known for the Zhuangzi, with its butterfly dream, Cook Ding and the useless tree
  • Emblem the swallowtail butterfly

The life

Zhuangzi, whose given name was Zhuang Zhou, lived in the Warring States period, when the old Zhou order had broken apart and rival kingdoms fought one another for mastery. Traveling advisers, later remembered as the Hundred Schools, went from court to court offering rulers a cure for the disorder. Confucians urged ritual and rank, Mohists argued for impartial benefit, and Legalists called for strict law and harsh punishment. Zhuangzi proposed no program for governing at all.

Very little about him is certain. The main early source is a short notice by the historian Sima Qian, written some two hundred years after his death. It places him in Meng, in the small state of Song, says he once held a minor post overseeing a lacquer garden, and traces the essentials of his thought to Laozi. The usual dates, about 369 to 286 BCE, are estimates.

Everything else comes from the book that carries his name. The Zhuangzi as it survives has thirty-three chapters. Scholars generally credit only the first seven, the Inner Chapters, to Zhuangzi himself. The Outer Chapters (8–22) and the Miscellaneous Chapters (23–33) were added by later writers working in his manner. Several of the best-known stories about him come from those later chapters, so they show how his followers remembered him more than they record what he did.

In those stories he is poor and untroubled by it. He goes to see the King of Wei in ragged clothes and straw sandals. He is offered high office and turns it down. His closest companion is Hui Shi, a logician known for his paradoxes, and their exchanges run through the book as a friendly, unending contest between argument and something argument cannot settle. In one, Zhuangzi remarks on the pleasure of the fish swimming below them, and Hui Shi asks how he can know what a fish enjoys.

When Zhuangzi's wife died, chapter 18 relates, Hui Shi came to offer condolences and found the widower squatting with his legs flung out, beating time on a tub and singing. Hui Shi protested that this went too far. Zhuangzi answered that he had grieved like anyone at first. Then he had looked back to a time before she had a body, before she even had a spirit, and saw her birth and death as changes like the turning of the four seasons. To keep on wailing, he said, would show he had not understood.

Zhuangzi founded no school and left no institution. His book, with the Tao Te Ching, became one of the two founding texts of Taoism. Its humor, its parables and its distrust of fixed categories later fed into Chan Buddhism in China and Zen in Japan, and into centuries of Chinese poetry and painting.

Core teaching

The Zhuangzi treats the distinctions people take as fixed, such as useful and useless, right and wrong, life and death, as the work of a particular point of view rather than features of the world itself. A gnarled tree that no carpenter will touch lives to a great age because its wood is worthless. A man unfit for soldiering is passed over when others are taken off to war. The best-known image comes in chapter 2: Zhuangzi dreams he is a butterfly, wakes, and cannot tell whether he is a man who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being a man. He leaves the question open. Alongside this loosening of categories runs a practical ideal, wu wei, action that does not force. In chapter 3, Cook Ding carves oxen so deftly that his blade stays sharp for nineteen years, because he cuts along the natural gaps instead of hacking at bone. Freedom, in this picture, comes from moving with the grain of things until the effort, and the one making it, seem to fall away.

A story told about him

Chapter 17 of the Zhuangzi tells that he was fishing on the Pu River when two officials arrived from the King of Chu. The king wished to make him his chief minister. Zhuangzi kept hold of his rod and did not turn his head. He had heard, he said, that Chu kept a sacred tortoise, dead for three thousand years, its remains wrapped in cloth and enshrined in a temple. Would that tortoise rather be dead and honored, or alive and dragging its tail through the mud? Alive, the officials agreed, and in the mud. Then go away, Zhuangzi told them. He would drag his tail in the mud.

Questions to sit with

  1. Zhuangzi woke from a dream and could not say whether he was a man or a butterfly, and he left it there. What is gained by living with a question instead of settling it?
  2. In his stories the useless tree outlives the useful one. Where can being judged useless offer a kind of shelter or freedom?
  3. Offered high office, he chose to stay a tortoise in the mud. What would you turn down to keep your own freedom, and what would it cost?
