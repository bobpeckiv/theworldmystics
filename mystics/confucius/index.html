Confucianism · c. 551–479 BCE · state of Lu, China

Confucius

A teacher from the small state of Lu, Confucius took pupils from any family, searched in vain for a ruler to serve, and became the founding figure of Confucianism.

“Yu, shall I teach you what knowledge is? When you know a thing, to hold that you know it; and when you do not know a thing, to allow that you do not know it;—this is knowledge.”

Confucius, Analects 2.17, James Legge, 1893

At a glance

  • Born around 551 BCE, by the traditional dating, in the state of Lu, in present-day Shandong province, China
  • Died 479 BCE, in Lu; buried at Qufu
  • Tradition Confucianism, of which he is the founding figure
  • Known for the Analects, the sayings and conversations gathered by his disciples and their students
  • Emblem the bundle of dried meat

The life

Kong Qiu, honored in China as Kongzi, "Master Kong," and known in the West by the Latinized name Confucius, came from Lu, a minor state on the eastern edge of the Zhou world. His traditional birth date, about 551 BCE, comes from the historian Sima Qian, writing some four centuries later. By then the Zhou order had worn thin. The king kept his title and little else, the dukes of Lu were overshadowed by a few great clans, and the old ceremonies were often kept up by people who no longer meant them.

His father, an elderly military officer, died while Kong Qiu was still small, and the family was poor. In the Analects he credits his humble start for his many practical skills. As a young man he worked as a steward for a leading Lu family, minding granaries and herds. He married, and a son, Kong Li, was born.

Then he began to teach, and in a new way. Training in the rites, music and the old writings had belonged to the households of the nobility. Confucius took whoever came, saying he had never refused instruction to anyone who brought as little as a bundle of dried meat. A few dozen disciples gathered around him, and the Analects keeps their characters alive: Yan Hui, poor and absorbed in learning; Zilu, blunt and brave; Zigong, rich and quick with words; and Zai Wo, rebuked for sleeping through the day.

Sima Qian says he held office in Lu around 501 BCE, perhaps as Minister of Crime; historians are cautious about the details. He left Lu around 497 BCE; the reasons are told in more than one way. For roughly fourteen years he moved among Wei, Song, Chen, Cai and other states, hoping some ruler would act on his advice. A crowd at Kuang mistook him for another man, and an official in Song is said to have tried to have him killed. On the border of Chen and Cai the provisions gave out, and his followers grew too weak from hunger to stand. No ruler took him into service.

Around 484 BCE he returned home and gave his last years to teaching. Losses followed quickly. Kong Li died, then Yan Hui, whose death drew from Confucius the cry that Heaven was destroying him, and then Zilu, killed in fighting in Wei. He died in 479 BCE, and his grave lies at Qufu.

The Analects was assembled by his disciples and their own students over several generations, and its later books are generally thought to be later additions. Tradition credits him with editing the classics, which most modern scholars doubt. Later Taoist writers, in the Zhuangzi above all, made him a character in their own parables, and tradition tells of a meeting with Laozi. A few centuries after his death the Han dynasty adopted his school as the official learning of the state, and for some two thousand years his teaching shaped education, government and family life across East Asia.

Core teaching

Confucius held that a well-ordered society begins with cultivated people, and that cultivation happens in ordinary conduct. Two words carry much of the teaching. Li covers ritual and propriety, from offerings to the ancestors to the proper way to greet a guest or mourn a parent; performed sincerely, it is the form through which people honor one another. Ren, often rendered "humaneness" or "goodness," is the quality that ritual expresses and trains. Asked for a single word to guide a whole life, he offered shu, reciprocity: never treating others in a way one would refuse to be treated. His disciples remembered him as free of fixed opinions, rigid plans, stubbornness and self-regard. He had little to say about spirits or death and steered such questions back toward the living, yet he spoke of Heaven, Tian, with reverence. Whether Confucianism is best called a religion or a philosophy is still argued. Some read it as a humane ethics with no need of the supernatural; others, pointing to his reverence for Heaven and the ritual life around him, read it as a religious path that finds the sacred within everyday life.

A story told about him

The Analects records an afternoon when Confucius sat with four disciples and asked what each would do if a ruler ever recognized his worth. Zilu answered first, promising to set a troubled state in order within three years. A second hoped to make a small territory prosperous, and a third asked only to assist modestly at ceremonies. The fourth, Zeng Dian, had been playing a stringed instrument. He set it aside and said that at the end of spring, in the light clothes of the season, he would go out with a few friends and some boys, bathe in the river, enjoy the breeze by the rain altars, and walk home singing. Confucius sighed and said that he was with Zeng Dian.

Questions to sit with

  1. Confucius held that courtesy and ceremony, done sincerely, are how people show one another that they matter. Which ordinary forms in your own life still carry that weight, and which have gone hollow?
  2. For some fourteen years he sought a court that would employ him and never found one, yet his teaching outlasted every court he visited. How should a life's work be judged when its effects arrive long after?
  3. He declined to speculate about spirits or death and turned his listeners back toward the living. Is that reticence a kind of faith, a kind of doubt, or something else?
