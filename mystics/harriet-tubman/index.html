Harriet Tubman

Born enslaved on Maryland's Eastern Shore, she escaped alone, then went back again and again for her family, guided by visions she took as God's voice.

At a glance

  • Born Dorchester County, Maryland, around 1822
  • Died Auburn, New York, March 10, 1913
  • Tradition Christianity, in the African American Methodist tradition
  • Known for leading enslaved people to freedom on the Underground Railroad, and guiding the Combahee River raid in 1863
  • Emblem the North Star

The life

Araminta Ross, called Minty, was born around 1822 in Dorchester County, on Maryland's Eastern Shore, to Harriet "Rit" Green and Ben Ross, both enslaved. As a small child she was hired out to a man named James Cook, who sent her wading into cold water to check his muskrat traps until she fell sick and was sent home. Another employer whipped her before she was old enough to understand why. Three of her older sisters were sold south, and the family lived waiting for the next sale.

At about thirteen she was struck in the head by a two-pound weight an overseer threw at someone else, and her skull was cracked. From then on she lived with crushing headaches, spells of sleep that took her without warning, and vivid dreams and visions. Historians today link them to the injury. She understood them as God speaking, and she acted on what they told her.

Her owner died around 1849, and the people on his estate faced being sold. She left. Two of her brothers set out with her, grew frightened, and turned back, and she went back with them. Then she left again, alone, and walked to Pennsylvania.

She did not stay there. Over roughly thirteen trips into Maryland she brought out about seventy people herself, among them her parents and her brothers, and gave directions that helped dozens more escape on their own. After the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 made the free states unsafe, she led people all the way to St. Catharines, in Ontario. She traveled at night by the North Star, sang to signal when it was safe to come out, and relied on Quaker households and free Black sailors. The Quaker stationmaster Thomas Garrett said he had never met anyone with more confidence in the voice of God.

During the Civil War she scouted for the Union Army. In the early hours of June 2, 1863, she guided gunboats up the Combahee River in South Carolina, past mines her own network of scouts had located, and more than seven hundred enslaved people ran from the rice fields down to the boats and to freedom. Afterward she spent decades pressing the federal government for a pension it was slow to pay, and she ran a home for elderly and poor people on her own property in Auburn, New York. She died there, poor, in 1913.

Core teaching

Tubman left no writings and no doctrine; she never learned to read. What survives is a set of reported sayings and stories, and they share one pattern: she made no separation between spiritual guidance and practical action. She took her dreams and visions as God's direction and acted on them, on the road north and in the war. Her prayers, as they were recorded, are blunt and direct, closer to talking with a partner than to petitioning a distant power. Her account of reaching Pennsylvania adds a second point. Freedom for herself alone did not feel complete while her family was still enslaved, and that conviction drove every trip she made back into Maryland.

A story told about her

When she first crossed into Pennsylvania, she later told Sarah Bradford, she held up her hands and studied them, checking whether freedom had made her someone else. It had not. The difference lay outside her: the sun came through the trees and over the fields like gold, and she felt as if she were in heaven. The feeling did not last. No one was waiting to welcome her, and her family was still in Maryland. That was when she decided they should be free too, and that she would bring them north. Tubman could not read or write, so this story survives through Sarah Bradford's books, first published in 1869 and retold in 1886.

Questions to sit with

  1. The first thing Tubman did when she was free was look at her own hands. What might she have been checking for, and why might it matter that the change she noticed was in the world around her rather than in herself?
  2. She was already safe, and she went back into danger about thirteen times. What makes a person go back for others once they have made it out?
  3. Everything Tubman said survives through people who wrote it down for her. How should that change the way her words are read?
