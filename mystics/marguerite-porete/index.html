Christianity · d. 1310 · Hainaut to Paris

Marguerite Porete

A laywoman who answered to no abbot, she wrote a book in French that a bishop burned, kept sharing it anyway, and stayed silent before her judges.

At a glance

  • Born Hainaut, in the Low Countries, in the mid-1200s
  • Died the Place de Grève, Paris, June 1, 1310
  • Tradition Christian mysticism, in the Beguine movement
  • Known for The Mirror of Simple Souls, a book on the soul's surrender to God, written in Old French
  • Emblem the hand mirror

The life

Almost nothing is known of Marguerite Porete's early life except that she came from Hainaut, a county on what is now the border of France and Belgium, and that she became a Beguine, an independent one attached to no beguinage. The Beguines were laywomen who lived by prayer and work among the poor without taking vows or entering a convent. They answered to no abbot, and church leaders of her time distrusted them for it.

She wrote one book, The Mirror of Simple Souls, in Old French. Writing in the language of the people rather than in Latin put its ideas within reach of readers who had no clerical training.

Sometime between 1296 and 1306, the Bishop of Cambrai, Guido of Collemezzo, condemned the book and had it burned in public at Valenciennes. She went on sharing it. She sent it to three churchmen for their judgment, among them Godfrey of Fontaines, a leading Paris theologian, and none of them found it dangerous. She defied the next Bishop of Cambrai and a local inquisitor as well.

She was arrested and brought to Paris, where William of Paris, a Dominican inquisitor and confessor to King Philip IV, took her case. He was also prosecuting the Knights Templar. She refused the oath that would have opened her questioning and stayed silent in prison for about a year and a half. A panel of twenty-one theologians condemned articles taken from her book, chiefly the claim that the soul brought to nothing is freed from the virtues. Another prisoner, Guiard of Cressonessart, was tried in the same proceedings. On June 1, 1310, weeks after fifty-four Templars were burned outside the city, she was burned in the Place de Grève.

Her book was burned too, but copies survived. For six centuries it was read without her name, translated into Middle English, Latin and Italian, copied in monasteries, and sometimes credited to the Flemish mystic John of Ruusbroec. Meister Eckhart, who came to Paris to teach the year after her death, taught ideas close to hers. In 1946 the scholar Romana Guarnieri matched the condemned articles to the surviving text and gave the Mirror back its author.

Core teaching

Her book is written as a dialogue in which Love, who is God, answers the Soul and a scandalized Reason, and its central claim concerns the will. Marguerite Porete taught that love can draw a soul into God so fully that it keeps no will of its own: whatever it consents to is what God wills in it. Her word for such a soul is anientie, brought to nothing. She kept one line firm. God is God by nature; the soul is joined to God by love, never by nature.

She traced the road in seven states. The first three are the familiar labors of keeping the commandments, giving up comforts and doing good works; the fourth is an absorbing contemplative love that leaves outward works behind, though the soul is still the one doing the loving. The fifth is the giving up of the will, the sixth a union in which the self drops from view and only God is seen, and the seventh a glory she held could not be told before death. The soul in the later states lives sans nul pourquoi, without any why, no longer serving for reward or salvation.

A story told about her

The story goes that when Godfrey of Fontaines, one of the most respected theologians in Paris, read the Mirror, he did not call it heresy. He is reported to have said that a soul cannot reach the divine life the book describes until it reaches the practice the book teaches. Marguerite had asked the experts for a ruling after a bishop burned her book, and the most learned of them answered that it was describing something real. A few years later, other theologians in the same city read sentences cut out of the same book and condemned it.

Questions to sit with

  1. Marguerite wrote in French instead of Latin, the language of the clergy. How does the language a book is written in decide who gets to join the conversation about it?
  2. She stayed silent for a year and a half rather than answer her judges. Is refusing to speak a kind of strength, or does it give up the chance to defend yourself?
  3. Her book was read for six hundred years without her name on it. Does it matter who wrote a book, as long as its ideas survive?
