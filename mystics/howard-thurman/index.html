Christianity · 1899–1981 · Daytona to San Francisco

Howard Thurman

The grandson of an enslaved woman, he co-founded a San Francisco church open to every race and faith and wrote a book that shaped Martin Luther King Jr.

At a glance

  • Born Florida (probably West Palm Beach), November 18, 1899
  • Died San Francisco, California, April 10, 1981
  • Tradition Christianity, in the African American church, with an interfaith reach
  • Known for co-founding the Church for the Fellowship of All Peoples in 1944, and writing Jesus and the Disinherited (1949)
  • Emblem the oak

The life

San Francisco, 1944. A Black minister walked away from a secure job to help start a church meant from its first Sunday to gather people of every race and faith into one congregation, which almost no American church had tried. He was Howard Thurman, and his partner in the work was a white minister, Alfred Fisk. They called it the Church for the Fellowship of All Peoples. Thurman was a Floridian, born in 1899 and raised in Daytona on the Black side of a segregated town.

The idea behind that church reached back to his grandmother. Nancy Ambrose had lived through slavery and never learned her letters, so the boy did the reading when she wanted scripture. She drew one line. Paul's letters were not to be read to her, apart from the love chapter of First Corinthians now and then, since plantation preachers had quoted Paul as proof that she was owned. In his place she handed Howard a memory of the hidden worship of the enslaved, where a preacher would tell the gathering that no master truly owned them and that each of them was a child of God. Thurman built on that declaration for the rest of his life.

Church itself nearly lost him first. When he was seven, his father, Saul, died of pneumonia. The traveling preacher brought in for the service had never known Saul, and he told the mourners the dead man was in hell, as a lesson for anyone not yet converted. The boy turned his back on that church. For years he looked for God outdoors instead, and an oak tree in the family yard became the friend he confided in.

He found his way back through study. He finished Morehouse College in Atlanta, then Rochester Theological Seminary in New York, where Black students were rare, and went on to preach and teach. In 1935–36 he led a delegation to India, and in February 1936 he met Mohandas Gandhi (that story is told below). Later he served as dean of Marsh Chapel at Boston University; no Black scholar had held that kind of post at a mostly white American university before him.

In 1949 he published Jesus and the Disinherited. It pictured Jesus as a poor Jew in a land ruled by Rome, one of a despised minority, and asked what his teaching could offer the people the world presses hardest. Thurman's answer was that it guards the inner life against fear, deception and hatred. Martin Luther King Jr. is said to have carried the book with him. Thurman seldom spoke from a rally stage. Organizers sought his counsel in private, and he urged them toward silence first, a practice he called "centering down," and then back to the work. He died in San Francisco on April 10, 1981.

Core teaching

At the center of Thurman's thought is "the sound of the genuine": the belief that in every person there is an inner voice, the presence of God, which is the truest thing about them and their one reliable guide. Learning to hear it in oneself and to honor it in others was, for him, the whole of the spiritual task. Because that inner voice is the same in everyone, he held that all life is one, and he read segregation as a denial of that unity. He tied the inward search to public life. In Jesus and the Disinherited he presented the teaching of Jesus as a way for people under pressure to keep fear, deception and hatred from taking hold of them. His method was "centering down," a descent into silence, followed by a return to action, which he treated as a single movement rather than two.

A story told about him

In February 1936, at Bardoli in western India, Thurman and Mohandas Gandhi talked for close to three hours. Near the end, Gandhi offered a prediction: that the clearest form of nonviolence might one day go out to the world by way of Black Americans. Gandhi's secretary, Mahadev Desai, took notes and published an account in Harijan the next month; Thurman told the story again, from memory, in his autobiography, With Head and Heart, in 1979. What followed is on the record. He went home to a segregated country and spent the next four decades on one question, how people under a system built to diminish them keep their souls whole, and the leaders of the movement that answered Gandhi's prediction came to him to talk it through.

Questions to sit with

  1. Gandhi predicted that nonviolence might spread from Black Americans to the rest of the world. Why might an idea born in one country need people in a very different situation to carry it further?
  2. Thurman's practice was to go quiet before he acted. When you face a hard choice, what helps you think clearly, and how can you tell the difference between taking time to think and avoiding the choice?
  3. Civil rights leaders went to Thurman for advice away from the cameras. Whom do you go to when you need counsel you can trust, and what makes someone that kind of person?
