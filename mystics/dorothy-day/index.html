Catholic Christianity · 1897–1980 · Brooklyn to New York City

Dorothy Day

A radical reporter who went to jail for the vote became a Catholic, opened her door to anyone hungry, and kept it open for nearly fifty years.

At a glance

  • Born Brooklyn, New York, November 8, 1897
  • Died New York City, November 29, 1980
  • Tradition Catholic Christianity, in the Catholic Worker Movement
  • Known for co-founding The Catholic Worker and its houses of hospitality, and a lifetime of pacifist protest
  • Emblem the bowl

The life

In 1926, in a beach cottage she shared with Forster Batterham, Dorothy Day gave birth to a daughter named Tamar. Day later wrote that the birth filled her with a gladness so large it became a wish to worship. She had Tamar baptized Catholic, and in December 1927 she entered the Church herself. Forster would not follow, and they separated. Her friends on the left called it defection.

They had known a different woman. Born in Brooklyn on November 8, 1897, Day was writing for radical papers before she was twenty, and living in Greenwich Village among Communists and playwrights. In 1917 she was locked up with the suffragists who picketed the White House for the vote, and behind bars she went on hunger strike with them. Under it all sat an emptiness no cause could fill, and years later it named her autobiography, The Long Loneliness.

Faith gave her a direction but no task, and for years she looked for a way to be Catholic and radical at once. In 1932 Peter Maurin, a French peasant-philosopher, arrived at her door. Believers, he argued, should not wait on the state; they should feed the hungry and take in the homeless themselves, and so build a world where goodness came more easily. Together they launched The Catholic Worker on May 1, 1933, at a penny a copy, in the depths of the Depression. Before long the paper's office doubled as a soup kitchen.

From that kitchen came the houses of hospitality. Anyone who arrived got a meal and a bed, and nobody had to qualify, convert or move on by a deadline. The idea spread to more than a hundred communities. Day ran it from the inside, sleeping in the same buildings and eating the same soup as her guests, with no salary and almost no possessions. She believed the person in the breadline was Christ himself. She also loved opera, the sea and Dostoevsky's novels, and she held that delight was a duty, an idea she took from John Ruskin that later gave her diaries, published in 2008, their title: The Duty of Delight.

She died on November 29, 1980, at Maryhouse, a Catholic Worker house on the Lower East Side, and homeless guests and famous admirers crowded her funeral side by side. Many knew her as much for her refusals as for her soup. Her pacifism allowed no exceptions, and her stand against the Second World War cost the paper most of its readership and shut many houses. Through the 1950s she was arrested repeatedly for refusing to go underground when New York held its mandatory air-raid drills, staying out in City Hall Park to protest the nuclear arms race.

Core teaching

Day's faith rested on one conviction: the hungry person at the door was Christ, not a stand-in for him. She read the Gospel teaching that what is done for the least is done for Christ as a plain description, and so she saw no line between worship and service, or between religion and politics. Daily Mass and the breadline were, for her, parts of one practice, and she could not say where prayer stopped and work began. She found God less in private experience than in community, in a meal shared with people the wider society had cast aside, and her autobiography closes on that theme. She also held that love is something done, not only felt. That conviction led to voluntary poverty: she ate, slept and lived alongside her guests and owned no more than they did. She often cited a passage from Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov setting the hard, costly love of action against the easier love of daydreams, and she wanted it credited to him rather than to her.

A story told about her

In the summer of 1973 the United Farm Workers were picketing California's vineyards, and Dorothy Day, seventy-five and frail, went to stand with them. Near Lamont, when sheriff's deputies faced the picket line, she met them sitting on a small folding stool on a dusty road. That same summer, in Fresno County, she was arrested with the strikers and held for more than a week. It was one more jailing in a record that began with the suffragists in 1917, and it was her last. Since her death the Church she joined at thirty has opened her cause for sainthood. She would have objected. She is widely remembered for warning admirers off the word saint, and as the story is usually told, she meant to stay a nuisance, not become a statue.

Questions to sit with

  1. Day believed that delight was a duty, even for people living among hardship. Can joy be a responsibility, or does it have to come on its own?
  2. Day took no salary and lived in the same buildings, on the same food, as the people she served. Does sharing someone's hardship make help better, or does it mostly change the helper?
  3. The houses of hospitality fed and sheltered anyone, with no conditions and no date to leave. What are the strengths of help given that way? What are the costs?
