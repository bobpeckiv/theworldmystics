Hinduism · c. 1130–c. 1160 · Udutadi to Srisailam

Akka Mahadevi

A twelfth-century poet from southern India who, the story goes, left a king's household, joined a movement that rejected caste, and signed every poem with her own name for Shiva.

At a glance

  • Born Udutadi, in what is now Karnataka, southern India, around 1130, by tradition
  • Died the Kadali forest near Srisailam, around 1160, by tradition
  • Tradition Hinduism, in the Virashaiva devotional movement
  • Known for several hundred short poems in Kannada, each ending with the name Chennamallikarjuna
  • Emblem the jasmine blossom

The life

By tradition, Mahadevi was born around 1130 in Udutadi, a village in southern India, to parents who worshipped the god Shiva. From childhood she gave her devotion to Shiva as he was worshipped at Srisailam, a hill shrine far to the east, and she called him by a name of her own, Chennamallikarjuna, often translated "the lord white as jasmine." Tradition says she thought of him as her husband even as a girl.

Her world was ordered by caste, the inherited rank that fixed a person's work, marriage and place in worship. Religious learning lived in Sanskrit, a language closed to her both as a woman and by birth. In her lifetime a movement called the Virashaivas, later known as Lingayats, pushed against all of it. Its members rejected caste, temple priests and image worship. Each wore a small emblem of Shiva on the body instead of going to a temple, and they taught in Kannada, the language people spoke at home. Tradition holds that at the town of Kalyana a leader named Basavanna gathered them in an assembly where a washerman, a cobbler and a Brahmin argued about God in the same room.

The story goes that a local king, Kaushika, wanted to marry her and broke the promises he made to win her, and that she left his household with nothing and walked to Kalyana. In the same telling, the assembly questioned her hard at the door before taking her in, and is said to have called her Akka, "elder sister," the name that stayed with her.

Her poems are called vachanas, which means "sayings." They are short and plain, closer to speech than to song, and each one ends with the name Chennamallikarjuna. That signature is how her poems are recognized today, and it also turns every poem into words spoken to her god rather than about him. Some are fierce and funny. In one she says husbands who grow old and die are worth no more than kindling. In another she names her in-laws: Maya, the power of illusion, is her mother-in-law, and the whole world is her father-in-law. Her later poems move from longing toward bayalu, "open space," where there is no longer anyone left to long.

In the traditional account, the Kalyana community held together for about a generation before it was scattered. Her poems outlasted it. Between three hundred fifty and four hundred thirty survive under her signature, and they are still recited and sung in Karnataka more than eight hundred years after her death.

Core teaching

In Akka Mahadevi's poems the closing signature carries the theology. Because every vachana ends by naming Chennamallikarjuna, each poem is addressed to the god and is never a description of him, and it closes the moment his name is said. Her language for that bond is marital, but she turns the usual comparison around. Earthly marriage is not the model that explains the divine one; in her poems the bond with Shiva is the real marriage and the human kind is a passing imitation. The Virashaiva practice behind the poems also removed distance. Each initiate wore an ishtalinga, a small personal emblem of Shiva, so there was no shrine to travel to and no priest standing between worshipper and god. The movement named its goal linga-anga samarasya, the oneness of the divine and the embodied person, sometimes rendered as two becoming one taste. Her later poems follow that aim toward bayalu, open space, where longing ends because no separate lover remains.

A story told about her

The tradition says that Mahadevi had spent everything to reach Kalyana, the one place that would accept her, and once there she did not stay. She left the assembly and went south and east to Srisailam, the shrine of the god she had loved since childhood, and into the Kadali forest below it, alone again. The story goes that she died there at about twenty-five, absorbed into the light of the god she had called her husband. No record confirms any of it. What remains are the poems, each one ending with his name.

Questions to sit with

  1. The Virashaivas replaced temples and priests with a small emblem each member wore on the body. What changes in a religion when the building and the priest are taken away?
  2. Akka Mahadevi used jokes, including one about mortal husbands, to make serious points. Is humor a fair way to argue about religion or marriage, or does it make serious ideas easier to dismiss?
  3. She reached the one community that welcomed her and then left it. Why might someone walk away from the place where they finally belong?
