American Transcendentalism · 1817–1862 · Concord, Massachusetts

Henry David Thoreau

A Concord pencil-maker's son who lived two years beside Walden Pond, spent a night in jail over a tax, and wrote some of America's most enduring books.

“The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation. What is called resignation is confirmed desperation.”

Henry David Thoreau, Walden, "Economy", 1854

At a glance

  • Born Concord, Massachusetts, July 12, 1817
  • Died Concord, Massachusetts, May 6, 1862, of tuberculosis, at forty-four
  • Tradition American Transcendentalism, the circle around Ralph Waldo Emerson; a close reader of the Bhagavad Gita, attached to no church in adult life
  • Known for Walden (1854), "Civil Disobedience" (1849), and a journal of some two million words kept for twenty-four years
  • Emblem the barred window

The life

He was christened David Henry Thoreau and later reversed the names on his own. Born in Concord on July 12, 1817, he was the third child of John Thoreau, a reserved maker of pencils, and Cynthia Dunbar Thoreau, who took in boarders and held firm antislavery views. He studied at Harvard from 1833 to 1837, then taught school in Concord with his older brother John. He also devised a better graphite mixture, and the Thoreau pencil came to be counted among the finest made in the country. He showed no wish to make a career of it.

The turning point came in the winter of 1842. On January 11 John died of tetanus, contracted from a small cut, with Henry beside him. Within days Henry's own jaw stiffened with the same symptoms, though no infection was ever found. Sixteen days after his brother's death, Emerson's five-year-old son Waldo, a boy Henry had grown fond of while living in the Emerson household, died of scarlet fever. Months later, in an essay for The Dial, Thoreau wrote, "Surely joy is the condition of life."

Emerson owned a woodlot on the shore of Walden Pond, roughly a mile and a half from the village toward the south, and let Thoreau use it. On July 4, 1845, Thoreau moved into a one-room house he had framed there with his own hands. He was no hermit. Trains passed along the opposite shore, and he went into Concord often. What he was testing was how simple a life could be made and what would come into view once the clutter was gone. He grew beans, read before sunrise, logged every expense, and studied the pond through the seasons. In July 1846 he was arrested for years of unpaid poll tax, which he had stopped paying in protest against slavery and the war with Mexico, and was held overnight in the village jail. He left the pond on September 6, 1847.

After the pond he worked as a land surveyor, helped run the pencil business, lectured, and walked the countryside daily. His first book, A Week on the Concord and Merrimack Rivers (1849), which recalls a boat trip with John, sold so poorly that most of the copies came back to him. Walden appeared in 1854. The Thoreau household sheltered people escaping slavery, and Henry himself helped some of them onto northbound trains. In 1859, after John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry, he spoke publicly in Brown's defense while many abolitionists held back. Not all of his record was admirable: in 1844 a cooking fire he and a friend had lit burned some three hundred acres of Concord woodland.

In December 1860 he caught a cold while counting tree rings out in the snow, and the tuberculosis long present in him flared. He kept editing his manuscripts through a slow decline and died at home on May 6, 1862. Emerson gave the eulogy.

Core teaching

Thoreau's central claim is that the sacred is not deferred to another world or a later time. In Walden he wrote that "God himself culminates in the present moment," and he treated close attention to the place where one already stands as a form of devotion. His method was simplification. Borrowing the language of the marketplace, he defined cost as "the amount of what I will call life" that must be given up for something, and he invited readers to judge their work and possessions by that measure. The word he chose for the pond experiment was "deliberately": a life lived on purpose, after weighing what mattered. The practice that carried the teaching for the rest of his life was daily walking joined to the journal, an exacting record of ice, birds, plants and weather that some readers compare to a contemplative office. In "Civil Disobedience" the same conviction turns outward: conscience, not law, is the final authority, and a person may refuse to cooperate with a government doing serious wrong.

A story told about him

The account of his night in jail comes almost entirely from Thoreau himself, in the essay that grew out of it. On a summer evening in 1846 he walked from the pond into the village to fetch a mended shoe, and Sam Staples, the town constable, stopped him over the poll tax he had not paid for several years. Thoreau would not pay, so Staples locked him up. His cellmate was a man held on suspicion of burning a barn. Thoreau listened to the village through the window and wrote later that the state, unable to reach his conscience, had settled for confining his body. Someone, probably an aunt, paid the tax, and in the morning he was let out, much against his will. He collected the shoe and, as he tells it, was picking huckleberries on a hill outside town before thirty minutes had passed. In 1907 Mohandas Gandhi, already leading Indian resistance in South Africa, read the resulting essay and wrote about it for his newspaper, and Martin Luther King Jr. first read it as a student at Morehouse College.

Questions to sit with

  1. Thoreau measured the price of things in hours of life rather than in dollars. What in your own life would look different if you counted its cost that way?
  2. He went to the pond to find out how little he needed, yet he walked into town often and ate at his family's table. Does a retreat have to be total to teach something?
  3. He was jailed for one night over an unpaid tax, and the essay that came out of it traveled further than he ever did. How should a single act of conscience be weighed when its effects come so much later?
