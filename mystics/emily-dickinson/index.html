American poetry · 1830–1886 · Amherst, Massachusetts

Emily Dickinson

A New England poet who would not claim a conversion she had not felt, she wrote nearly 1,800 poems at home in Amherst and saw about ten printed.

“So instead of getting to heaven at last, / I'm going all along!”

Emily Dickinson, "A Service of Song," Poems, ed. Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson, 1890

At a glance

  • Born Amherst, Massachusetts, December 10, 1830
  • Died Amherst, Massachusetts, May 15, 1886
  • Tradition American poetry, raised in New England Congregationalist Christianity
  • Known for nearly 1,800 short poems on death, doubt and the holy, almost all found only after she died
  • Emblem the sewn booklet

The life

Emily Dickinson was born on December 10, 1830, in the Homestead, her family's brick house on Main Street in Amherst, Massachusetts. Edward Dickinson, her father, practiced law, kept the books for Amherst College as its treasurer, and served one term in Congress. In Amherst, faith was public business. Revivals came through the town again and again in her youth, and each time neighbors rose in meeting, professed Christ and joined the church, while everyone kept count of who had come forward and who had not.

Her name stayed off those lists. At sixteen she entered Mount Holyoke Female Seminary, where Mary Lyon, who ran the school, sorted the students by the state of their souls. Emily ended the year among the thirty or so, out of about 235, still classed as having no hope. Back home, a revival in 1850 drew in most of her friends, and her sister Lavinia and her father joined the church as well. That April she wrote to a friend that she stood alone in rebellion. She never made the public profession, and by about 1860 she no longer attended services.

The great outpouring came next. Something frightened her badly in the fall of 1861, and she never said what. From 1862 through 1865 she wrote about 850 poems, many of them at a small cherry writing table in her corner bedroom. She sent more than 250 poems next door to her sister-in-law, Susan Gilbert Dickinson, her closest reader. Others she copied fair and stitched into forty small booklets, and she told no one about them.

Year by year her world narrowed to the house and garden. She dressed in white. By her forties very few people saw her at all, and those who called often talked with her around the edge of a door. The town called her the Myth. Yet over a thousand of her letters survive, many written to comfort grieving neighbors. She nursed her mother through seven years in bed, and late in life she carried on a romance by letter with Judge Otis Phillips Lord, an old friend of her father's. Then the deaths came close together: her father in 1874, her mother in 1882, her eight-year-old nephew Gib in 1883, Lord in 1884.

She died in Amherst on May 15, 1886, at fifty-five. Her last note, to two cousins, said little more than "Called back." After the funeral Lavinia came upon the booklets: nearly 1,800 poems, of which only about ten had ever appeared in print, unsigned and changed by editors. A first selection came out in 1890 and went through eleven printings in two years.

Core teaching

Dickinson left no doctrine, and her religious thought has to be read out of the poems and letters. It begins with a refusal. She would not testify to an inner change she had not undergone, so she stayed outside the profession her town treated as the mark of salvation. In her work doubt is not the end of religious attention but one of its forms. In "This World is not Conclusion" the confident claims of faith keep running up against a hunger that nothing quiets. God appears less as a figure described than as a pressure felt at the edges of ordinary things, such as a slant of winter afternoon light or the stillness that follows a death. Her dashes and abrupt endings leave such questions deliberately open. Beside this runs a quiet domestic piety. In the poem her first editors printed in 1890 as "A Service of Song," the Sabbath is kept in the garden instead of a pew, and heaven becomes less a place reached at the end than a road already underway.

A story told about her

The story often told about her first letter to Thomas Wentworth Higginson is about how she gave her name. In the spring of 1862 she had read his advice to new writers in the Atlantic Monthly, and she sent him four poems with a short note. The note had no signature. She wrote her name on a small card, sealed the card inside its own smaller envelope, and slipped that envelope in with the letter. A stranger had to open two envelopes to learn who was writing. Higginson answered, and they kept writing to each other for years. In 1869 she told him that he had saved her life without knowing it.

Questions to sit with

  1. Dickinson saw almost no one in person for years, yet over a thousand of her letters survive, many sent to people in grief. Can you be close to someone you rarely see?
  2. Her neighbors called her the Myth. What happens when a town turns a person it does not understand into a legend, and what gets lost?
  3. She wrote most during a stretch of fear she never explained to anyone. Can making something help a person carry what they cannot talk about?
