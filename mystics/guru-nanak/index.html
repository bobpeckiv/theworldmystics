Sikhism · 1469–1539 · Talwandi to Kartarpur, Punjab

Guru Nanak

Born in a Punjab village in 1469, Guru Nanak spent two decades on the road singing of one God beyond every creed and founded the community that became Sikhism.

“He who understandeth God's order, O Nanak, is never guilty of egoism.”

Guru Nanak, Japji, pauri 2, Max Arthur Macauliffe, 1909

At a glance

  • Born 1469, in Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib, in the Punjab province of present-day Pakistan
  • Died 1539, at Kartarpur, on the Ravi River
  • Tradition Sikhism, as its founder and the first of its ten Gurus
  • Known for hymns preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib, among them the Japji; the langar, the free communal kitchen
  • Emblem the rebab

The life

Nanak was born in 1469 at Talwandi, a village in the Punjab now called Nankana Sahib, in Pakistan. His father, Mehta Kalu, was the village revenue accountant, or patwari, from the Khatri caste. The region then belonged to the Delhi Sultanate under its Lodi rulers. Hindus and Muslims lived as neighbors, yet each community tended to regard the other as astray. Caste ranked Hindu society from birth and could decide whose touch defiled food, and the scriptures of both faiths were kept in Sanskrit and Arabic, tongues few villagers understood.

Much of what is known of his life is drawn from the janamsakhis, devotional life stories written down by later generations of Sikhs, which often disagree with one another. They describe a boy more interested in wandering holy men than in the family's affairs. He married Sulakhni, and they had two sons, Sri Chand and Lakhmi Das. As a young man he moved to Sultanpur, where he kept the storehouse of the local governor.

At about thirty, the stories say, he went to bathe in the Bein, a small river near Sultanpur, and vanished. He reappeared three days later, and the words remembered from his return were that there is no Hindu and no Muslim. The versions disagree on what happened in between. Sikh tradition understands it as the moment he received his calling from God.

He gave up his post and set out on some twenty years of travel, much of it on foot, with Mardana, a Muslim musician who played the rebab. Traditional accounts describe four great journeys, through much of India and, in the stories, on to Tibet, Sri Lanka, Mecca and Baghdad. At each stop Mardana played and Nanak sang hymns in Punjabi, the speech of ordinary people, in praise of one God beyond every sectarian name. In 1526 the armies of Babur, founder of the Mughal empire, swept through the region, and several of Nanak's hymns grieve over the cruelty of that conquest.

In his fifties he settled on farmland at Kartarpur, beside the Ravi River, and worked the land himself. There a community grew up around him, and with it the langar, a shared kitchen where Brahmin and outcaste, Hindu and Muslim, sat side by side on the floor and shared one meal. In a society that policed who could eat with whom, the meal was itself a teaching.

Before his death in 1539 he chose a disciple, Angad, to lead the community, passing over both of his sons. A well-known story says his Hindu and Muslim followers quarreled over whether to cremate or bury him and found only flowers beneath the sheet; a similar story is told of the poet Kabir. Nine Gurus followed him. His hymns, gathered with theirs into the Guru Granth Sahib, compiled in 1604, are sung daily in gurdwaras around the world, and most gurdwaras still offer food to every visitor without charge.

Core teaching

At the heart of Guru Nanak's teaching is one God, named in the opening words of the Sikh scripture, Ik Onkar, which Sikhs often translate as "One Universal Creator." That God is formless, beyond birth and death, and present in every person, and no caste, priesthood or sacred language stands between God and anyone who seeks. The path he taught is naam simran, the steady remembrance of the divine Name, carried into daily life rather than kept for the temple. Its obstacle is haumai, self-centeredness, which ends as a person comes to understand and accept hukam, the divine order that runs through all things. Sikhs often sum up the practical side of his teaching in three duties: remember God, earn an honest living, and share with others. He set conduct above doctrine. In Sant Singh Khalsa's English translation of the Guru Granth Sahib, one of his hymns says that truth is higher than everything, "but higher still is truthful living."

A story told about him

One of the best-known janamsakhi stories places Guru Nanak in Mecca. Tired from the journey, he lay down to rest with his feet toward the Kaaba. A local official woke him in anger and demanded to know how he dared point his feet at the house of God. Nanak did not argue. He asked the man to turn his feet toward some direction where God was not. The wording differs from source to source, and the visit cannot be confirmed, but the point of the story has stayed constant for five centuries: no direction is empty of God.

Questions to sit with

  1. In the stories, the first words Nanak spoke after the river were that there is no Hindu and no Muslim. Which of the labels you carry might fall away in that light, and what would be left?
  2. The langar placed people of every caste and creed on the same floor to share a meal. What changes when equality is practiced with the body instead of argued for?
  3. Asked to turn his feet away from God, he asked where God was not. Where do you draw lines between holy places and ordinary ones, and what are those lines for?
