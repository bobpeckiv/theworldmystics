American wilderness writing · 1838–1914 · Dunbar, Wisconsin and the Sierra Nevada

John Muir

A Scottish-born naturalist raised on a strict Bible-reading frontier farm, John Muir found his church in the Sierra Nevada and spent his later life defending it.

“We are now in the mountains and they are in us, kindling enthusiasm, making every nerve quiver, filling every pore and cell of us.”

John Muir, My First Summer in the Sierra, journal entry for June 6, 1869, none (written in English), 1911

At a glance

  • Born Dunbar, Scotland, April 21, 1838
  • Died Los Angeles, California, December 24, 1914
  • Tradition American wilderness writing, raised in Scottish evangelical Calvinism and then among the Campbellites
  • Known for My First Summer in the Sierra, The Mountains of California and Our National Parks; the campaign for Yosemite National Park; cofounding the Sierra Club
  • Emblem the hitch knot

The life

John Muir grew up on the North Sea coast at Dunbar, Scotland, the third of eight children of Ann Gilrye and Daniel Muir, a grain merchant of fierce piety. Daniel joined the Campbellites, a restorationist sect, and in 1849 took the family to a homestead in Wisconsin. Work ran from before light until after dark, and scripture came with the rod; by John's own count he knew most of the Bible "by heart and by sore flesh." Allowed to get up as early as he wished, he was up by one o'clock each night, reading and building clocks and other machines. His inventions, shown at the Madison state fair in 1860, opened the way to the University of Wisconsin, which he left in 1863 without a degree.

In March 1867, at a carriage-parts shop in Indianapolis, a tool slipped and struck his right eye, and for weeks he could barely see with either one. When sight returned he turned away from industry. In his own later telling, he resolved to devote his life "to the study of the inventions of God." That autumn he walked and botanized south to the Florida coast, where malaria laid him low. In March 1868 he reached San Francisco.

The Sierra Nevada became his home and his study. In 1869 he went into the high country as overseer for a sheep drive, keeping the journal that became My First Summer in the Sierra. Climbing Cathedral Peak alone that September, he wrote that he had been "at church in California" for the first time. He argued that glaciers, not a sudden collapse, had shaped Yosemite Valley, against the state geologist Josiah Whitney, and in 1871 he located a glacier still moving. That year Ralph Waldo Emerson visited him in the valley.

In 1880 Muir married Louie Wanda Strentzel and ran her family's fruit ranch at Martinez, traveling in Alaska with Tlingit canoe guides between harvests. Articles he wrote after an 1889 camping trip with the editor Robert Underwood Johnson helped bring about Yosemite National Park in 1890. Two years later he was a founder of the Sierra Club, and he was its president until his death. President Theodore Roosevelt camped with him for three nights in 1903. Louie died in 1905. His fight to keep Hetch Hetchy Valley from becoming a San Francisco reservoir was lost with the Raker Act of December 1913, and a year later he died of pneumonia.

His record has a harder side. The valley he described as untouched wilderness was the home of the Ahwahneechee, whose villages the Mariposa Battalion had burned in 1851, and its open forests owed much to their long use of fire. His early writings portray Native people, and Black people met on the southern walk, in demeaning stereotypes, which the Sierra Club acknowledged publicly in July 2020. In later years he wrote admiringly of the Tlingit and spoke of the kinship of all peoples. Both parts of the record stand.

Core teaching

Muir left no system, but his books return to one conviction: the natural world is a single interwoven whole, and God is present in it rather than behind it. He rejected the idea, common in his day, that creation exists for human use, and defended alligators and rattlesnakes as having their own place in the Creator's design. For him close scientific observation and worship were the same act. Measuring a glacier or tracing the path of a storm was a way of attending to the divine. His practice was immersion: walking, climbing and living outdoors for long stretches with very little gear, and going out into storms rather than sheltering from them. The theology was inherited and reshaped. He kept the language of his Calvinist upbringing and the cadence of the King James Bible while moving the place of worship from the meetinghouse to the mountains. In his later years this conviction became public argument. In The Yosemite he wrote that "everybody needs beauty as well as bread," and he treated the defense of wild places as the protection of sacred ground.

A story told about him

Muir told this story himself in The Yosemite. Early one March morning in 1872, at about half-past two, an earthquake shook him awake in his cabin in Yosemite Valley. Instead of taking cover, he ran out into the moonlit meadow calling out "A noble earthquake!" He was sure, he said, that he was about to learn something. Where his neighbors in the valley saw danger, he saw a lesson in how the mountains were made, and he went out to meet it. Told and retold since, the night has become a small portrait of the man: glad of a force that sent others indoors, and too curious to stay in bed.

Questions to sit with

  1. Muir said he was in church on a bare mountain summit. What makes a place sacred to you, and must it have walls?
  2. He called Yosemite untouched at a time when its people had recently been forced out. How does a story about a place change once the people who lived there are written back into it?
  3. He moved between rapture and campaigning, from journals to lobbying Congress. When does wonder ask something of the one who feels it?
