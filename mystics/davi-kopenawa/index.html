Yanomami · born c. 1956 · Rio Toototobi to Watoriki

Davi Kopenawa

A Yanomami shaman from the Brazilian Amazon who learned the outsiders' language well enough to help win his people a protected homeland, and is still defending it.

At a glance

  • Born Marakana, upper Rio Toototobi, Amazonas, Brazil, around 1956
  • Died living
  • Tradition Yanomami shamanism, the work of seeing and calling the xapiri, the spirit-beings of the forest
  • Known for the long campaign that won the Yanomami a protected territory in 1992, and the book The Falling Sky
  • Emblem a woven basket

The life

Davi Kopenawa was born around 1956 in Marakana, a Yanomami communal house that sheltered some two hundred people on the upper Rio Toototobi, near Brazil's border with Venezuela. Outsiders reached the river when he was small. Disease came with them in two waves, in 1959 and 1967; the second, a measles epidemic, killed many of the people he had grown up among, his mother among them.

An American mission on the river gave him the name Davi and taught him Portuguese. He kept the language and left the religion. As a young man he went to work for FUNAI, Brazil's Indian agency, and, as he puts it, tried to become a white man. A bout of tuberculosis put him in the hospital for a long stretch. When Brazil drove the Perimetral Norte highway through Yanomami country in the mid-1970s, FUNAI used him as an interpreter along the construction line. He came to call the newcomers the People of Merchandise.

Around 1980 he married Fátima and settled at Watoriki, Wind Mountain, on the Rio Demini. Her father, Lourival, was the community's senior shaman and became his teacher. In the early 1980s, under Lourival's guidance and with yãkoana, a snuff made from tree bark, Kopenawa trained as a shaman. In his account, a shaman sees the xapiri, tiny shining spirits that dance down from the sky, and their dancing helps hold the sky up. If the shamans die out, he says, it will fall on everyone.

Then came the gold rush. From the mid-1980s, tens of thousands of prospectors poured into Yanomami country without permission, bringing malaria, and by Survival International's estimate about a fifth of the Yanomami in Brazil died between 1986 and 1993. Kopenawa carried the case to Brazil's presidents and Congress and on to Europe and the United Nations, which gave him its Global 500 award in 1988. In May 1992 Brazil formally marked out the Yanomami territory, an area roughly as large as Portugal.

The work did not end there. In 2004 he became the founding president of the Hutukara Yanomami Association. Over many years of recorded conversations with the French anthropologist Bruce Albert, he told the story of his life and his people's understanding of the world, published in French in 2010 and in English in 2013 as The Falling Sky. In 2019 he received the Right Livelihood Award. When illegal mining surged again, the federal government declared a health emergency across the Yanomami territory in 2023. He still lives at Watoriki.

Core teaching

In Kopenawa's account, the forest is not raw material that happens to carry spiritual meaning. It is alive throughout, home to the xapiri and to the images of the first ancestors, so the split between nature and spirit that outsiders assume does not hold. In his view "ecology" is a newcomer's word for something his people had no reason to name, since they never set the forest apart from daily life. Shamans come to know this through yãkoana, taken in ceremony, under which they see the xapiri come down and dance. Wisdom, as he describes it, grows from that seeing, repeated over many years, rather than from the study of a text. At the center is the image that gives his book its title. An earlier sky once collapsed, and the present one stays in place because living shamans and their spirits keep holding it up. Without them, he warns, the collapse would come again, this time for everyone, outsiders included. Destroying the forest is therefore, in his telling, a breach of the terms on which the world was first given. He told this to Bruce Albert so that outsiders could hear it in their own languages.

A story told about him

In an interview with the anthropologist Terence Turner, Kopenawa described the worst of his people's situation: disease spreading, help slow to come, too few shamans left. Turner asked why he kept fighting if things were that hopeless. Kopenawa did not answer with a plan or a promise that the fight would be won. He said he fought because he was alive, and for his people, children and adults alike, so that they would not have to die so young.

Questions to sit with

  1. Kopenawa is alive and still speaking for himself. How is reading about a living person different from reading about someone who died centuries ago? What does a reader owe a person who can still answer back?
  2. He learned Portuguese from a mission and used it to defend a way of life the mission had hoped to change. What happens when a tool is turned to a purpose its givers never intended?
  3. In his account, the sky stays up because people keep doing their part to hold it. What else in the world only lasts as long as someone keeps working to hold it up?
