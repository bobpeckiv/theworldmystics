Islam · c. 1315–1390 · Shiraz, Persia

Hafiz

A Qur'an scholar of fourteenth-century Shiraz who wrote of wine, roses and the Beloved, Hafiz made the Persian ghazal a vehicle for Sufi longing and for mockery of hypocrisy.

“Last night I dreamed that angels stood without / The tavern door, and knocked in vain, and wept; / They took the clay of Adam, and, methought, / Moulded a cup therewith while all men slept.”

Hafiz, Divan, ghazal 184 (Bell, Poems from the Divan of Hafiz, no. XXXIV), Gertrude Bell, 1897

At a glance

  • Born around 1315 in Shiraz, in southern Persia (present-day Iran); sources range to about 1325
  • Died 1390, or 1389 by some accounts, in Shiraz
  • Tradition Islam, in the Sufi poetic tradition; he names no master and joined no order
  • Known for the Divan, about five hundred ghazals, still opened in Iranian homes as a book of omens
  • Emblem the closed door

The life

He was born Shams al-Din Muhammad in Shiraz, the chief city of the province of Fars, around 1315. His father, a merchant named Baha al-Din in most accounts, died while he was young. A later story sets him to work in a bakery; no contemporary record supports it. The pen name he chose is firmer evidence. Hafiz was the title of someone who had learned the whole Qur'an by heart, and in one verse he claims to recite it in all fourteen of the traditional readings.

Unlike most Persian poets of his age, he spent nearly his whole life in one city. Shiraz had submitted to the Mongols in the previous century and been spared, and after the Ilkhanate broke apart in the 1330s it passed between rival local dynasties. While still in his twenties Hafiz was known at the court of Abu Ishaq Inju, a ruler remembered for his love of poetry and pleasure.

In 1353 the Muzaffarid Mubariz al-Din Muhammad took Shiraz. Abu Ishaq fled and was executed in 1357. The new ruler was severely pious: taverns were shut, music was forbidden and officers enforced public morals. His son Shah Shuja is said to have mocked him in a quatrain as the city's muhtasib, its inspector of morals, and Hafiz seems to have taken up the jibe in his own verse. In 1358 Shah Shuja deposed his father and had him blinded, the taverns reopened, and Hafiz spent most of his remaining years under the son's patronage, not always in favor.

No account of his life was set down while he lived. Personal details come from the poems and from lives of the poets compiled long afterward, which add stories of journeys, bereavements and a meeting with the conqueror Timur that historians treat as legend.

He died in Shiraz in 1390, or by some accounts 1389. Tradition says that clerics objected to giving a poet of wine a Muslim burial, and that the matter was settled by drawing a line of his verse at random: the line told mourners not to hold back from the bier of Hafiz, for though sunk in sin he was bound for Paradise. He was laid in the Musalla gardens by the Ruknabad stream, places his poems praise. His tomb there is still visited.

His Divan was gathered after his death; tradition credits the work to a friend, Muhammad Golandam, though the authorship of its preface is disputed. Families in Iran open it at random to put a question to it, a practice called fal-e Hafiz, and he is known as Lisan al-Ghayb, the Tongue of the Unseen. A German translation of 1812–13 inspired Goethe's West-östlicher Divan (1819).

Many of the most widely shared poems published under his name since the late 1990s, by the American author Daniel Ladinsky, are modern compositions. Ladinsky describes them as renderings, not translations, and critics have found no Persian ghazals behind them. Older versions such as Gertrude Bell's (1897) stay closer to the text.

Core teaching

At the center of Hafiz's poetry is a deliberate doubleness. Wine, the tavern, the cupbearer and the Beloved can be read as earthly things or as the language Sufis had long used for the soul's intoxication with God, and his ghazals seldom settle which. Commentators have argued the point for six centuries; many readers take the refusal to choose as the teaching itself, a view in which the holy and the ordinary were never two. A recurring image is the cup of Jamshid, the legendary king's vessel in which the whole world could be seen. In one well-known ghazal the heart searches for years and begs from strangers a treasure it already holds. Against this runs his steady target, riya, piety performed for an audience. The preacher, the ascetic in showy wool and the morals officer come off worst in the Divan, while the rend, the disreputable but honest drinker, is the figure he favors, because the rend does not pretend. Beside the satire runs consolation: the refrain gham makhor, "grieve not," has been passed between mourners for centuries.

Questions to sit with

  1. Hafiz wrote poems that could be read as tavern songs or as prayers, and would not say which. What is gained, and what is risked, by leaving a meaning open?
  2. He kept his sharpest words for piety put on for show. Where is the line between keeping a practice private and keeping up appearances?
  3. Families still open his book at random to ask about their lives. What does it mean to let a poem answer a question?
