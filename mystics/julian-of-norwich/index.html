Christianity · c. 1343–after 1416 · Norwich

Julian of Norwich

An anchoress walled into a cell beside a Norwich church, she survived a near-fatal illness in 1373 and spent decades writing what its visions had shown her.

“It behoved that there should be sin; but all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well.”

Julian of Norwich, Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 27, Grace Warrack, 1901

At a glance

  • Born probably in or near Norwich, England, around 1343
  • Died after 1416, the last year she appears in the records
  • Tradition Christianity, as an English anchoress before the Reformation
  • Known for Revelations of Divine Love, written in English, in a Short Text and a Long Text
  • Emblem the hazelnut

The life

Almost nothing is known of her family or her early years. She is called Julian after the Church of St Julian in Norwich, where she later lived enclosed, and her own name has probably been lost. She was born around 1343, probably in or near Norwich, then one of England's largest cities, and grew up in a century of repeated catastrophe. The Black Death struck the city around 1349 and returned in later waves. The Peasants' Revolt of 1381 brought bloodshed to the region. In 1401 England passed De heretico comburendo, the first English statute that sent heretics to the fire, directed above all at the Lollards.

By her own account, as a young woman she had asked God for three gifts: a deeper understanding of the Passion of Christ, an illness grave enough that she would expect to die of it, and three inward "wounds" of contrition, compassion and longing for God. In May 1373, when she was thirty and a half, the illness came. She received the last rites, and her curate was sent for so that he could be present at her end. He held a crucifix up before her face. As her sight dimmed she kept her eyes on it, and she later wrote that she saw blood flowing from beneath the crown of thorns. Sixteen revelations followed, which she called "showings." Her text dates them to May 13: fifteen came between about four and nine that morning, and the last on the following night. Then she recovered.

She gave the rest of her life to working out what the showings meant. A first account, now called the Short Text, was written either soon afterward or, as some scholars argue, in the 1380s. A far longer version, the Long Text, came after twenty years or more of reflection and may not have been finished until the 1410s. The two are the earliest surviving English writings that can be shown to come from a woman. She wrote in English rather than Latin, for ordinary believers she called her "even-Christians," at a time when a woman writing theology in the common tongue drew suspicion.

At some point she was enclosed as an anchoress in a cell built onto St Julian's; a will of 1394 is the earliest record of her there. The rite of enclosure treated the anchoress as dead to the world. Her cell had a window into the church, through which she received the sacrament, and one onto the street, where people came to ask for counsel. In the early 1410s the visionary Margery Kempe traveled to Norwich to consult her and described the visit in The Book of Margery Kempe. When Julian died is not recorded.

For centuries the book was copied quietly, much of it in convents, and was little known. Grace Warrack's modernized edition of 1901 helped bring it to a wide readership, and T. S. Eliot later wove her line "all shall be well" into Four Quartets.

Core teaching

Julian's central claim is that God is love and holds no wrath toward the soul. The soul, she wrote, is not outside God trying to find a way in: "In Him we are enclosed, and He in us" (ch. 54, ed. Grace Warrack). Her best-known image is the hazelnut. Shown something small and round resting in her hand, she understood that it was everything that has been made, and that it goes on existing only because God loves it. She did not deny sin or pain. She held that sin was in some way necessary, and that it, along with all suffering, is contained in a love whose end is well-being; this is the sense of her most quoted words, that all shall be well. Her language is homely and bodily, full of clothing, enclosure and nursing, and she spoke of Christ as Mother as readily as of God as Father. The teaching came to her through a body she thought was dying, and she offered it to every believer, not only to clergy and scholars.

Questions to sit with

  1. Julian lived through plague, revolt and the first burnings of dissenters in England, and still wrote that all shall be well. Is that kind of hope a turning away from suffering, or a way of seeing it whole?
  2. She saw everything that exists as something small and fragile enough to rest in one hand. What changes when the vast is pictured as small and held, rather than mighty?
  3. She chose a life inside one small room, yet people sought her out for counsel. What can withdrawal from the world make possible, and what does it cost?
