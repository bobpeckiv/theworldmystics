Judaism · c. 1698–1760 · Podolia and Medzhybizh

The Baal Shem Tov

Born Israel ben Eliezer to a poor family in eastern Europe, he taught that God fills every place and is served with joy; Hasidism grew from his circle.

At a glance

  • Born around 1698 by tradition (many historians give about 1700), traditionally in Podolia, now western Ukraine; historians dispute both the year and the place
  • Died 1760, in Medzhybizh
  • Tradition Judaism, as the founding figure of Hasidism
  • Known for teaching that God is present in all things and is served with joy; he wrote almost nothing, and his teachings come through his students
  • Emblem the fire in the forest

The life

Almost everything known about Israel ben Eliezer comes from his students and from later collections of stories, and historians separate a modest documented core from a large body of legend. He was born around 1698 by the traditional reckoning, though many historians prefer about 1700, in a poor village traditionally placed in Podolia, in the southeastern Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. Both the year and the place are disputed. He lost his parents early, and tradition says his dying father told him to fear nothing but God.

The Jewish communities of the region were poor and still marked by catastrophe. In 1648–49 the Khmelnytsky uprising had killed tens of thousands of Jews, and a generation later the messianic movement around Sabbatai Zevi had ended in his conversion to Islam. Religious standing belonged to the learned, the men who had mastered the Talmud, and most working people had little access to that world.

The young man supported himself with humble work. He served as a behelfer, an assistant who brought young pupils to and from their lessons, kept a synagogue, dug lime and clay for sale and helped at an inn that his wife ran. The legends add years of solitary prayer in the Carpathian mountains. He also became a baal shem, a "master of the Name," one of the folk healers of the time who treated the sick and the troubled with prayer, herbs and divine names. The title by which he is remembered, Baal Shem Tov, marks him as a good one; it is often shortened to the Besht.

By the traditional account he began teaching openly at about thirty-six. Unlettered villagers and trained scholars alike were drawn to him. Among his followers were Dov Ber, later called the Maggid of Mezeritch, who led the movement after him, and Jacob Joseph of Polonne. Around 1740 he settled in Medzhybizh, where he lived until his death in 1760. Accounts from his circle describe his prayer as intensely physical, with swaying, trembling and a burning fervor called hitlahavut, and with wordless melodies, niggunim, sung as prayer.

He left almost nothing in writing. His teachings survive in what his disciples recorded: the anthology Tzava'at HaRivash, which also holds teachings of Dov Ber; the collection Keter Shem Tov; and Toldot Yaakov Yosef (1780), a Torah commentary by Jacob Joseph that quotes him often and was the first Hasidic book to be printed. A collection of wonder-tales, Shivchei HaBesht, followed in 1814–15. Modern historians, notably Moshe Rosman and Immanuel Etkes, have worked to tell the historical figure apart from the legendary one.

Within a few decades of his death, Hasidism had spread across much of eastern Europe. It remains a living movement, and its communities around the world trace their spiritual lineage to him.

Core teaching

At the center of the Baal Shem Tov's teaching is divine immanence: the conviction that God's presence fills the whole world, with no place, act or person left outside it. He took Isaiah's words, "the whole earth is full of His glory," as a literal description, and he treated the felt distance from God as the real exile. From this followed avodah b'gashmiyut, worship through physical life. Eating, working, trading and dancing could all become devekut, cleaving to God, when done with awareness of the presence within them, so holiness did not require leaving ordinary life behind. The second pillar is joy. Against a climate of melancholy and harsh self-denial, he taught that gladness, song and dance lead to God more surely than grief. One teaching in Tzava'at HaRivash holds that "prayer with great joy is certainly much more acceptable before [God], blessed be He, than prayer in sadness and with weeping" (§107, trans. J. Immanuel Schochet). Together these ideas opened the life of the spirit to people without learning, who had felt shut out of it.

A story told about him

A story told in Hasidic tradition is set on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement. The Baal Shem Tov and a crowded congregation had prayed all day, and the prayers, the story says, would not rise. Among the people was a herd boy who could not read the prayer book. Wanting to pray and having no words, he took out the small whistle he used in the fields and blew a single loud note. Those around him were outraged that the holy day had been broken. The Baal Shem Tov, in the telling, saw it otherwise: the boy's note had lifted every prayer in the room. The tale is legend rather than record, and its details shift from one telling to the next; in some the whistle is a flute. It is remembered because it puts his central claim into a single scene: before God, a whole heart counts for more than learning.

Questions to sit with

  1. The Baal Shem Tov held that ordinary work and meals could be a form of worship. What would change if the most routine hour of a day were treated as something sacred?
  2. He taught joy to communities that had lived through massacre and poverty. Is joy in the face of suffering a denial of it, or a kind of courage?
  3. In the whistle story, one untrained note outweighs a day of learned prayer. What is gained, and what might be lost, when sincerity is valued above knowledge?
